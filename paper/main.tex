\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A \emph{packet scheduler} decides which of the packets buffered at an output port leaves next.
On a programmable switch, that decision belongs to the network operator rather than to the silicon.
Operators use that freedom to isolate tenants from one another and to hold latency-sensitive traffic to its budget.
The policies they write are usually hierarchical~\cite{Stephens19,Kumar19,Grant20,vPIFO}, and each level of that hierarchy is a scheduling decision in its own right.
Whatever the policy, the scheduler has to reach its decision at line rate, since a scheduler that cannot keep up with the wire drops the very traffic it was meant to schedule.
The abstraction that makes this affordable in hardware is the \emph{PIFO}, a priority queue that a switch can maintain at line rate~\cite{Sivaraman16}.
A hierarchical policy runs on a tree of PIFOs, one queue per node.

A deployment's policy is not fixed for the life of the deployment.
Traffic patterns shift, tenants arrive and leave, and telemetry from downstream tells the operator that the policy in force is no longer the right one.
Such changes need to be rapid and seamless.
The scheduler, however, was compiled for the policy it is running.
Its tree shape, its per-node disciplines, and its table contents are all artifacts of a decision made before the switch was brought up, and the compilers that produce them accept \emph{a policy to run} rather than \emph{a change to the policy already running}.

Consider a small-office gateway running \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\), strictly prioritizing real-time \textsf{zoom} traffic over \textsf{gmail} traffic.
The network operator wants to add a new \textsf{spotify} flow, and is considering two candidate policies:
\(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{mid}{:}\,\textsf{spotify},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\), extending the strict-priority list with \textsf{spotify}; or
\(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify}))\), keeping \textsf{zoom} at the higher tier but having \textsf{gmail} and \textsf{spotify} share the lower tier via round-robin.
In either case, the state of the art~\cite{vPIFO} would stop the world: drop or recirculate the buffered packets, recompile, and reinstall.
The costs are downtime, delay, and the rebuilding of nodes that did not need to be rebuilt.

This paper refuses to stop the world.
Our thesis is that the interval during which a scheduler is being reprogrammed is itself a scheduling policy.
Stopping the world does not do away with that policy but only leaves it unstated and unchosen.
Understanding this unstated policy is a language problem.
A transitionary policy needs a syntax that the operator can read, a semantics that says which packet leaves next, and a lowering onto hardware that was built for the policy it is already running.
With those in hand, the question of whether a transition was carried out correctly becomes a theorem about the semantics rather than a measurement of the hardware.
Rio supplies all three: a language of policy edits, a semantics for them, and a lowering of them onto a PIFO substrate.

A transition from a running policy \(p_1\) to a requested policy \(p_2\) takes one of two shapes (\cref{fig:two-timelines}).
Sometimes the requested policy can be installed immediately, as a single atomic transition (case~1).
Sometimes it cannot, and the scheduler must first pass through a transitionary policy (case~2).
Our two candidates illustrate the two cases.

Moving to \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{mid}{:}\,\textsf{spotify},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\) is a case~1 transition.
Before time \(t_1\), \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\) is running; at time \(t_1\) the request to transition is received, and we immediately satisfy it.
The transition at \(t_1\) is \emph{atomic}, meaning that any interaction that the network had with the scheduler before \(t_1\) happened entirely in the old regime, and whatever interaction the network had with the scheduler after \(t_1\) happened entirely in the new regime.
It is also \emph{immediate}, in that no period of preparation precedes the atomic jump into \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{mid}{:}\,\textsf{spotify},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\).
The two concepts are distinct.

Moving to \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify}))\) is a case~2 transition.
As before, \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\) runs before \(t_1\) and the operator's request arrives at \(t_1\), but we cannot move to \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify}))\) immediately since there are still \textsf{gmail} packets buffered in the scheduler.
Transitioning immediately would mean re-sorting those buffered \textsf{gmail} packets into the new round-robin leaf, but their scheduling order was fixed the moment they were enqueued; we cannot rewrite that past without dropping or reordering them.
We atomically step instead into a \emph{transitionary policy} that is \emph{not} the one the operator asked for.
After \(t_1\) but before \(t_2\), the scheduler still accepts and emits packets under it.
At time \(t_2\), defined as the instant that a well-defined guard becomes true, we atomically step into \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify}))\).

The operator never specified the semantics of the interval from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\), but that interval is a packet scheduling regime all the same.
It is unavoidable, it persists for nonzero time, and throughout it the scheduler goes on accepting and emitting packets, so scheduling decisions are being made in it.
Some of those decisions are better than others from the operator's point of view, and the state of the art has settled on a trivial one, discarding the backlog and starting over~\cite{vPIFO}.
Our contributions are to say what the transitionary policy is and to do better than that.

Rio's language is a grammar of atomic policy edits, each one a change the hardware can install in a single instant (\cref{sec:grammar}).
Its semantics gives every edit a meaning in the operator's own policy language, so \emph{any transitionary policy the grammar produces is ordinary scheduling}, readable exactly as \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are (\cref{sec:edit-semantics}).
A planner turns a requested \(p_2\) into a guarded sequence of those edits (\cref{sec:planner}), and a mechanical lowering carries each edit down to the instructions that a switch runs (\cref{sec:hardware}).
A transition from any \(p_1\) to any \(p_2\) is always available, and many admit a realization confined to the part of the tree that actually changes.
We will give the semantics of the stop-the-world transitionary policy and use it as a baseline.
Two practical goals guide the search for better ways to transition from a given \(p_1\) to a given \(p_2\).
We would like to shorten the transition period, and we would like to avoid dropping or delaying packets.

\section{Background \& Motivation}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Hierarchical scheduling on hardware}\label{sec:hierarchical}

A \emph{PIFO} (push-in, first-out queue) is a priority queue that is additionally defined to break rank-ties in FIFO order.
Where a single PIFO expresses just one scheduling decision, a PIFO tree composes policies via parent-child relationships, capturing the hierarchical policies that operators actually deploy.

A PIFO tree is typically lowered to hardware by mapping each tree depth onto a dedicated processing element (PE), with siblings and cousins sharing the PE at their depth.
Pinning a depth level to a single PE has well-studied benefits for computation and storage, and it is the layout \citet{Sivaraman16} introduced.
The PE budget is fixed by the silicon, so the running tree's shape is constrained by what the deployed PEs can host.


\subsection{vPIFO, and the problem it leaves open}\label{sec:vpifo}

\xxx[AM]{Not sure if they get their own section}

vPIFO~\cite{vPIFO} is the closest related work, and it explicitly leaves our problem open.
It virtualizes a single physical PIFO into many logical ``PIFO instances,'' with a Scheduling Description Language (SDL) and compiler, so their PIFO Visor can \emph{flexibly establish} hierarchical PIFO trees of arbitrary shape on fixed hardware; its contribution is the reconfigurable substrate.
As published, however, vPIFO has no notion of a difference between old and new policies, no formal semantics for what a policy change means, and no account of in-flight packets during a change.
Their SDL IR computes ranks and is compiled to P4 or to a CPU.
The two grammars we introduce in \cref{sec:grammar} are structural instead: one describes the shape of a policy tree, and the other describes how that shape changes.

Our two running examples make the gap concrete.
In vPIFO's own terms, the Operation Generation Table and the PIFO Instance Address Table encode the running tree's shape and its per-instance storage.
The easy change, to \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{mid}{:}\,\textsf{spotify},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\), is the kind of edit vPIFO's substrate is architecturally close to absorbing.
It over-provisions logical instances and its Visor can assemble them into arbitrary tree shapes, so a targeted append to those tables would register the new traffic and its operations while leaving the running instances untouched.
vPIFO exposes no such edit at runtime, however: those tables are generated offline by its SDL compiler, so installing even this easy change on a running scheduler would require extending the substrate with an online-patch path it does not have today.
The harder restructuring, to \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify}))\), lies beyond what the vPIFO substrate can absorb in place.
It changes the running tree's shape, inserting a new internal \RR{} node and re-parenting \textsf{gmail} under it, and neither table exposes that as an in-place edit.
The closest vPIFO comes is a full reinitialization onto the new shape, which is the stop-the-world baseline \cref{sec:intro} refuses.
vPIFO names runtime updating as future work, observing that the scheduling of packets during a modification's transitional phase is unresolved.

We are not competing with vPIFO and do not claim a better PIFO substrate.
We supply the layer \emph{above} a PIFO substrate: the formal transition between two policies, the small patch that realizes it, and the transitionary semantics.
The two layers can plausibly compose, and \cref{sec:hw-substrate} sets out the minimal requirement our contributions place on a substrate.

\section{Policies and Controls}\label{sec:model}

This section introduces the two objects that the rest of the paper edits.
\cref{sec:policy-dsl} gives the small DSL an operator writes, \cref{sec:controls} gives the control a switch runs, and \cref{sec:compilation} compiles the first into the second.

\subsection{What the operator writes}\label{sec:policy-dsl}

An operator wants to say which flows exist and how they should share the link.
A switch runs something much lower level, a tree of queues carrying scheduling logic at every node, which \cref{sec:controls} makes precise.
We start on the operator's side, with a small policy DSL \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}, and \cref{sec:compilation} compiles it down.

\begin{figure}[t]
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\mathit{flow} \in F\\[4pt]
D\ \text{a scheduling discipline, declaring a metadata set } M_{D}\\[8pt]
\mathit{pol} \mathrel{::=} \mathit{flow} \;\mid\; D(m_1{:}\,\mathit{pol}_1, \ldots, m_n{:}\,\mathit{pol}_n),\ m_i \in M_{D}
\end{array}
\]
\caption{The policy language \(\mathit{pol}\).}
\label{fig:pol-grammar}
\end{figure}

The operator writes a \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} (\cref{fig:pol-grammar}).
It is either a leaf labeled by a flow, or an internal node running a scheduling discipline \ensuremath{D} over child policies.
Each arm carries a metadatum that tells \ensuremath{D} how to schedule it, drawn from a set \(M_{D}\) that \ensuremath{D} declares.
For instance, \(M_{\Strict}\) is a set of priorities and \(M_{\WFQ}\) a set of weights.
A discipline that treats its arms alike, such as \RR, declares a one-element set, and we elide that element when writing examples.
We read the arity off by counting children, so, e.g., \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\) is the 2-ary instance of the discipline \Strict.
Each leaf label denotes a \emph{flow}: a predicate over packets.

The family of disciplines is open.
\ensuremath{D} is any discipline equipped with the compilation recipe of \cref{sec:compilation}, and nothing that follows depends on which disciplines are available.
Our implementation instantiates \Strict, \RR, and \WFQ because they aid our exposition.

We also have the discipline \(\Strictstar\), which for the most part can be thought of as merely an instance of 2-ary \Strict{}.
While the operator may use \(\Strictstar\) in their policies, it is really designed to be introduced and removed by the compiler during an in-place subtree replacement, which \cref{sec:idioms} assembles as the \Replace{} idiom.
The two disciplines are told apart in \cref{sec:hardware}, where a novel optimization allows us to realize \(\Strictstar\) without a pifo or a tree level of its own.
That is what makes an in-place replacement smooth.

A \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} is written against a fixed \emph{flow universe} \(F\): a finite set of pairwise disjoint flow predicates declared by the operator before constructing the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}.
A \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} is \emph{valid} when (a) every discipline is applied at the proper arity, (b) every arm's metadatum is drawn from its parent discipline's set \(M_{D}\), and (c) the leaves form a partition of a subset of \(F\): each leaf carries a flow drawn from \(F\), and no two leaves carry the same flow.
A packet therefore matches at most one leaf's flow.
If a packet does not match any of the flows in the scheduling tree's leaves, the packet is dropped.

\subsection{The runtime model: controls}\label{sec:controls}

A \emph{control} is a PIFO tree in which every node carries its own rule for ranking what it enqueues.
The name and the model are from Mohan et al.~\cite{Mohan23}, and we note our departures from them as they arise.
A control is the concrete state a programmable switch executes at line rate, realizable on an FPGA or SmartNIC (\cref{sec:hardware} lowers a control to exactly such a target).

\paragraph{Syntax}

A control \(C\) is a finite tree whose every node carries a triple:
\[
C \mathrel{::=} \langle \mathit{state}, \mathit{pifo}, \mathit{z} \rangle(C_1, \ldots, C_n), \qquad n \geq 0.
\]
The subtrees \(C_1 \ldots C_n\) are the node's children and \(n\) is its arity.
A node with no children is a \emph{leaf}, and a node with children is \emph{internal}.
The three components of the triple are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} is a pair \((\mathit{node\_state}, \mathit{slot\_states})\): the scheduling discipline's per-node bookkeeping and a list of per-arm entries, one per child.
Both evolve as the node serves traffic.
When the node runs a discipline that keeps no per-arm bookkeeping, \ensuremath{\mathit{slot\_states}} is empty.
\item \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} is a PIFO of ranked \emph{payloads}.
A payload is what this node enqueues when presented with a packet.
At an internal node the payload is an index into the child list, and at a leaf it is the packet itself.
\item \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} is the node's \emph{scheduling transaction},
\[
\mathit{z} : \mathit{state} \times \mathit{Pkt} \rightharpoonup (\mathit{payload} \times \mathit{rank}) \times \mathit{state}.
\]
Given the node's \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} and an incoming packet, it returns the payload this node will enqueue for that packet, the rank to enqueue it at, and the node's updated \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}.
\ensuremath{\mathit{z}} is partial.
When it is undefined for a packet, nothing is enqueued at this node and \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} is unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The many roles of \ensuremath{\mathit{z}}: classifying, annotating, and ranking}

We write \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} as a single partial function, but it has three parts, and the edits of \cref{sec:grammar} rewrite them separately.
Mohan et al.\ instead keep one total transaction for the whole tree, returning an entire root-to-leaf path of indices and ranks, where ours is per-node and partial.

\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{classifier} answers whether this node accepts a given packet and where it sends it.
It has a \emph{service map}, written \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}\), which is a partial map from flows to payloads.  
At an internal node, \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}(f)\) is a unique child index, and following it brings a packet of flow \(f\) one step closer to its leaf.
At a leaf the payload is the packet itself, so the map is the trivial one on the single flow that leaf carries.
Its \emph{admitted set}, written \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit} \subseteq \mathrm{dom}(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves})\), is the subset of the flows that the node currently accepts.
\item The \emph{per-arm metadata}, written \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\), records what this node's discipline needs to know about each of its arms, such as priorities or weights.
A leaf has no arms, and so no metadata.
\item The \emph{ranker}, written \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{rank}\), is the scheduling logic that the node's discipline contributes.
Its inputs are the node's \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}, the chosen payload, and that payload's arm metadatum.
From this it mints the rank and the updated \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}.
A node's ranker names the discipline that the node runs.
\end{itemize}

So \(\mathit{z}(\mathit{state}, p)\) is defined exactly when \(\mathit{flow}(p) \in \mathit{z}.\mathit{admit}\). 
It then finds \(p\)'s payload in \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}\), and pairs that payload with the rank that \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{rank}\) computes.

\ensuremath{\mathit{z}} does not \emph{choose} the payload.
Distinct leaves carry distinct flows (\cref{sec:policy-dsl} makes this a validity condition), so a packet's flow already fixes the one leaf it is bound for.
That in turn fixes, at every node on the way down, which child the packet has to be handed to.
The service map reports that child rather than selecting it, and what \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} genuinely decides is the rank.

At an internal node the ranker sees the payload and not the packet, meaning that the node can discriminate only among its own children. 
It cannot do finer-grained discrimination based on packet- or flow-specific information. 
The node's behavior is therefore a function of its own arms alone, which is a win for modularity and composability.

\ensuremath{\mathit{state}} and \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} change as the node serves traffic, whereas \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} changes only when an edit rewrites it.
That is why the per-arm metadata sits in \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} rather than in \ensuremath{\mathit{slot\_states}}.
It is the configuration the operator chose, not bookkeeping that the traffic left behind.

\paragraph{\push{} and \pop{}}

A control exposes exactly two operations to the network:
\[
\begin{array}{@{}r@{\;}l@{}}
\push{} &: \mathit{control} \times \mathit{Pkt} \to \mathit{control},\\
\pop{} &: \mathit{control} \rightharpoonup \mathit{Pkt} \times \mathit{control}.
\end{array}
\]
A \push{} offers the packet in parallel to every node on its root-to-leaf path, and each of those nodes enqueues the payload its own \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} returns, at the rank \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} mints for it.
Ranks are fixed at \push{} time and never revised.
A \pop{} releases the best-ranked packet, resolving ``best-ranked'' from the top down.
It releases the best-ranked index at the root, follows that index to a child, recurses, and releases a packet once it reaches a leaf.
It consults \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} contents alone, never \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} or \ensuremath{\mathit{z}}.
These two are the \emph{only} observable interactions with a scheduler, which is why we frame an \emph{atomic} transition in terms of \push{}/\pop{} observability.

\paragraph{Well-formedness}

A control \(C\) is \emph{well-formed} (written \(\vdash C\)) when, at every internal node of \(C\), the \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} has, for each legal child index \(i\), exactly as many occurrences of \(i\) as there are packets stored in the leaf pifos of the subtree under the \(i\)-th child.
A control that fails this holds an index at some node with no packet under the corresponding child, so a \pop{} that follows that index down reaches a leaf with nothing to emit.

\paragraph{Addressing a node}

We address node-local components of a control by a \ensuremath{\mathit{path}}, a possibly empty sequence of child indices read from the root.
The local triple at the node reached by following \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} from control \(C\)'s root is written \(C@\mathit{path}\), with fields \(C@\mathit{path}.\mathit{state}\), \(C@\mathit{path}.\mathit{pifo}\), and \(C@\mathit{path}.\mathit{z}\).
We also write \(C@\mathit{path}.\mathit{node\_state}\) and \(C@\mathit{path}.\mathit{slot\_states}\) for the two components of \(C@\mathit{path}.\mathit{state}\).

\paragraph{What a discipline supplies}

\cref{fig:discipline} lists what has to be supplied to realize a discipline \ensuremath{D}: a metadata set \(M_{D}\), two state types of \ensuremath{D}'s own, and three operations.
Supplying those is the whole interface, and any \ensuremath{D} that supplies them is one Rio can compile.

\(\mathit{init\_node\_state}_{D}\) is the \(\mathit{node\_state}_{D}\) every \ensuremath{D}-node starts in, installed when a \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} is compiled into a control.
\(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}\) runs once per arm, at compile time and again whenever an edit splices a new arm under an already-running \ensuremath{D}-parent (\cref{sec:add}), taking the parent's current \(\mathit{node\_state}_{D}\) and the new arm's metadatum.

The three operations are opaque to us.
Rio names \(\mathit{init\_node\_state}_{D}\), \(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}\) and \(\mathit{rank}_{D}\), and never unfolds them.
The two state types are opaque in a stronger sense: nothing outside \ensuremath{D} builds or reads a \(\mathit{node\_state}_{D}\) or a \(\mathit{slot\_state}_{D}\), and Rio only ever appends, deletes and renumbers slot entries.
\(M_{D}\) is the exception, since the compiler checks an arm's metadatum against it (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}) and \ChangeMeta{} carries \ensuremath{D} so that the same check is possible on a form alone.
A discipline's interface is visible to us and its behavior is not.

Two obligations attend the exchange.
The first is path-consistency of admission: a packet's flow must be admitted at every node on that flow's root-to-leaf path, or at none of them.
This is a global property rather than a concern of any node-local \ensuremath{\mathit{z}}, and it is what keeps \(\vdash\) true under compilation and under every \(\delta\).
The second is that \(\mathit{rank}_{D}\) records which discipline the node runs, and not merely how that discipline ranks.
Suppose two disciplines happened to schedule identically on every input.
If a node stored only its ranking behavior, the two would be indistinguishable in a running control, and the policy we echo back to the operator could name the wrong discipline.
Recording \ensuremath{D} keeps them apart, which is what lets \cref{sec:equivalences} read the operator's own \ensuremath{D} off a running control.

\paragraph{From \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} to control}

We write \(\lceil p \rceil\) for the control compiled from \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} \(p\).
It has \(p\)'s tree shape, an empty \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} at every node, and \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} seeded by \(\mathit{init\_node\_state}_{D}\) and \(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}\).
Each node's \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} takes \ensuremath{D}'s ranker, copies each arm's written metadatum into \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\), and reads its classifier off the tree, sending every flow served beneath the node toward that flow's leaf.
A freshly compiled control therefore admits every flow it serves, and carries the operator's arm annotations verbatim.

This is essentially what the vPIFO paper's \emph{Scheduling Description Language} does informally~\cite{vPIFO}.
They do not pin down a grammar for SDL or formalize the compilation, so our DSL can be read as a formal core of their concrete language.

\section{A Grammar of Atomic Policy Edits}\label{sec:grammar}

\cref{sec:delta-grammar} fixes a grammar \(\delta\) of structural edits over the policy DSL of \cref{sec:policy-dsl}, admitting a grammatical form only when the hardware can install it atomically.
\cref{sec:equivalences} says when two policies, or two controls, count as the same, and how to read a policy off a running control.
\cref{sec:edit-semantics} gives each form its two readings.

\subsection{A Grammar for Policy Tree Edits}\label{sec:delta-grammar}

We fix a small grammar \(\delta\) of \emph{policy tree edits}, which are structural rewrites that transform one running policy tree into another.
Every form has two readings, pinned down form by form in \cref{sec:edit-semantics}.
\(\csem{\delta} : \mathit{control} \rightharpoonup \mathit{control}\) rewrites the running control, and \(\psem{\delta} : \mathit{pol} \rightharpoonup \mathit{pol}\) names that same edit's effect on the operator's policy in closed form.

Three properties shape the choice of forms.
They are \emph{atomic}, since a form is admitted only if the hardware substrate can commit \(\csem{\delta}\) as a single instant (\cref{sec:hardware}).
They are \emph{explainable}, since \(\psem{\delta}\) states every form's effect back to the operator in the DSL they wrote, so the planner can report which policy change it is about to make and echo it back for approval (\cref{sec:planner}).
And they are \emph{complete}, since the grammar can express a sequence that reaches any \(p_2\) from any \(p_1\), which \cref{sec:fallback} shows by exhibiting one that always works.

An edit describes \emph{where} in the tree the change lands and \emph{what} the change is.
Throughout this section \(p_1\) is the presently running policy, and \(p_1@\mathit{path}\) is its subtree at \ensuremath{\mathit{path}}.
The node a form's \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} names is its \emph{edit site}.

\subsection{Equivalences, and Reading Off the Running Policy}\label{sec:equivalences}

We write \(C \sim C'\) for the equivalence on well-formed controls that holds when the two are identical except in their live \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} contents and \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}s.
Two \(\sim\)-related controls thus have the same tree shape and the same \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} at every node, and differ at most in the transient live state that traffic leaves behind.
Serving \push{}/\pop{} operations is one way controls come to be \(\sim\)-related.

We write \(p \eqR p'\) for the smallest congruence on \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} such that, at any internal \ensuremath{D}-node, permuting the child arms gives a congruent \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}.
Each arm's metadatum is tagged to the arm, so it travels with the arm under the permutation.
The metadata is what carries the scheduling-meaningful content, so such a permutation does not change the scheduler.

Because \(\eqR\) only permutes sibling arms, each \(\eqR\)-class has a canonical representative under any fixed ordering of the arms at a node.

We write \(C \simR C'\) for the equivalence obtained by closing \(\sim\) under sibling permutations.
Permuting a node's children renumbers the slots they sit in, so the parent's \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} entries, its \ensuremath{\mathit{slot\_states}}, its \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}\), and its \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\) are all renumbered to match.
The two controls present different positional layouts and realize the same scheduler.


\paragraph{The bridge: \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\)}

We write \(\lfloor C \rfloor\) to mean ``the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} that \(C\) realizes''.
Intuitively, \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) reads the policy straight off the control.
It walks the control's tree and recovers, node by node, the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} term the control implements, discarding the live \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} and \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} contents that traffic happens to have left behind.
We define it by structural recursion on \(C\), with one clause for leaves and one for internal nodes.
Both clauses read out of \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} (\cref{sec:controls}).
A leaf is named by the flow its classifier serves.
An internal node is named by the discipline its ranker names, and its arms are annotated out of its metadata.

\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\lfloor C \rfloor = f,\ \text{when \(C\) is a leaf and } \mathrm{dom}(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}) = \{f\};\\[4pt]
\lfloor C \rfloor = D(m_1{:}\,\lfloor c_1 \rfloor, \ldots, m_n{:}\,\lfloor c_n \rfloor),\\
\qquad \text{when \(C\) is internal with children } c_1 \ldots c_n,\\
\qquad \mathit{z}.\mathit{rank} \text{ names } D, \text{ and } m_i = \mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}(i).
\end{array}
\]

The recursion runs on a finite tree and consults neither \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} nor \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} nor \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit}\), so it returns a single value.
\xxx[AM to AS]{You suggested defining \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) as the inverse of \(\lceil \cdot \rceil\), and then worried that \(\lceil \cdot \rceil\) is not invertible if one is strict about sibling order.
\(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) is now a structural recursion on \(C\), so it is total and single-valued by construction, and no inverse has to be argued for.
The three items below are then consequences rather than defining rules, and the third of them is a theorem.}
Every well-formed control therefore determines one \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}, independently of how that control was reached, and we may write \(\lfloor C \rfloor\) and use it as a function.

Three facts follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Compiler correctness.} Compiling a \(p\) to a control and reading it straight back recovers \(p\) itself, up to the sibling-order freedom the compiler enjoyed in laying it out: \(\lfloor \lceil p \rceil \rfloor \eqR p\).
This is immediate from the compilation of \cref{sec:compilation}, which installs \ensuremath{D}'s ranker at each internal node, copies each arm's annotation into \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\), and has each leaf serve its own flow.
The relation is \(\eqR\) rather than \(=\) precisely because the compiler can choose arm order when creating \(\lceil p \rceil\).
\item \emph{Insensitivity to live contents.} \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) reads only the tree shape, each leaf's served flow, and each internal node's ranker-discipline and per-arm metadata.
It never reads \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}, \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}, or \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit}\).
So \(\sim\)-related controls, which share shape and \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} outright, are equal when read off using \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\). 
Thus, if \(C \sim C'\), then \(\lfloor C \rfloor = \lfloor C' \rfloor\).
\item \emph{Closure under \(\delta\).} A form's two readings (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}) agree.
Editing the control and reading it off gives the same \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}, up to sibling order, as applying the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level effect to the read-off, so \(\lfloor \csem{\delta}(C) \rfloor \eqR \psem{\delta}(\lfloor C \rfloor)\).
This theorem is proved one form at a time in \cref{sec:per-form} and collected as \cref{thm:interplay}.
The relation is again \(\eqR\), not \(=\), because \(\csem{\delta}\) may lay the edited node's children out in any sibling order.
\end{enumerate}

This is how we give the transitionary policy the semantics that \cref{sec:intro} promises.
Even when the presently running \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} is just an intermediate policy that the runtime crafted on the way from the running policy to the operator's true request, we can still read off and report to the operator exactly what is running.
\cref{sec:interplay} takes up the third fact and proves it, as the ``commuting square'' these pieces trace out.

\subsection{Edit Semantics}\label{sec:edit-semantics}

This section gives each form of \(\delta\) its two readings.
\(\csem{\delta}\) says what the form does to the running control, and \(\psem{\delta}\) says what it does to the operator's policy.
Each is given here as a definition, together with the preconditions under which the form is defined.
That the two agree, and that neither disturbs the buffered backlog, are claims about the definitions rather than parts of them, and \cref{sec:correctness} proves both form by form.

\paragraph{Notation}

The \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level rules below read, overwrite, and shrink child lists.
A \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} node's child list \(\mathit{ts}\) holds \emph{annotated arms}, each a metadatum paired with a policy (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}).
We write \(\mathit{ts}[i]\) for the \(i\)-th arm, and \(\mathit{ts}[t/i]\) for \(\mathit{ts}\) with the \emph{policy} of its \(i\)-th arm overwritten by \(t\).
That arm keeps its annotation, and the arity does not change.
\(\mathit{ts}[-/k]\) drops the \(k\)-th arm, and later arms shift one slot to the left.
Indices follow the \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} convention of \cref{sec:delta-grammar}.

The control-level rules walk controls the same way.
Nodes are written \(\langle\mathit{state}, \mathit{pifo}, \mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs})\), as in \cref{sec:controls}, and we reuse \(\mathit{cs}[C'/i]\) and \(\mathit{cs}\mathbin{+\!\!+}[C']\) for the list operations on the child list \(\mathit{cs}\).

One invariant links a node's classifier to the tree, and every form below preserves it.
At any node, \(\mathrm{dom}(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves})\) is exactly the set of flows served by the leaves beneath it, and \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}\) sends each of them to the child that leads on toward its leaf.

A form's path names a target node, but it is often more convenient to work with the target's parent and the index the parent uses to reach it.
We recover both by splitting the given path as \(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k]\): \(\pi\) is the path to the parent, and \(k\) is the child index by which the parent reaches the target.
We also order paths by prefix.
\(\nu \sqsubset \tau\) says that \(C@\nu\) is a proper ancestor of \(C@\tau\).
The loose version \(\nu \sqsubseteq \tau\) allows \(\nu = \tau\) as well.

Most forms edit only the node that their path points to.
For such a form we use a small combinator: given a node transformer \(g\) and a path \(\pi\), write \(\mathtt{at}(\pi, g)\) for the operation that walks to the node at \(\pi\), applies \(g\) there, and copies every other node of the tree unchanged:
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\mathtt{at}([\,], g)\,(N) = g(N)\\
\mathtt{at}(i \mathbin{::} \mathit{rest}, g)\,\langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}) =\\
\quad \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}[\,\mathtt{at}(\mathit{rest}, g)(\mathit{cs}[i]) / i\,])
\end{array}
\]
The recursive case walks one step down and writes the result back, carrying \(\langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle\) unchanged.
The same combinator serves on \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}, where a node is \(D(\mathit{ts})\).
There the descent into arm \(i\) rewrites that arm's policy and carries its annotation along.
We also write \(\mathtt{at}(V, g)\) for the set analogue, applying \(g\) at every node in \(V\) and copying every node outside \(V\) unchanged.

Three operations recur across the forms, and the first two edit a single node's \ensuremath{\mathit{z}}.
A node's discipline fixes a packet's rank from the local \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} and the chosen child alone, not from the packet itself, so admitting a flow at an already-running node calls for no new rank rule.
\(\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z}, F, i)\) therefore touches the classifier and nothing else, recording that child \(i\) is where \(F\) is served and adding \(F\) to the admitted set.
\(\mathtt{setmeta}(\mathit{z}, i, m)\) annotates arm \(i\) with \(m\) and leaves the classifier and the ranker alone:
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z}, F, i).\mathit{serves} = \mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}\,[\,f \mapsto i \;\text{for}\; f \in F\,],\\[3pt]
\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z}, F, i).\mathit{admit} = \mathit{z}.\mathit{admit} \cup F,\\[3pt]
\mathtt{setmeta}(\mathit{z}, i, m).\mathit{meta} = \mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\,[\,i \mapsto m\,].
\end{array}
\]
Every other field of \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} is carried through unchanged.
A form that admits a new set of flows must extend every node on the way down to its target, so \(\mathtt{extend}\) is also applied as a walk.
At each proper ancestor \(C@\nu\) of the target \(C@\tau\) the walk replaces \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} by \(\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z}, F, i)\), where \(i\) is the index by which \(\nu\) continues toward \(\tau\), and it copies every other node unchanged.

\Add{} comes first and is given in full detail.
The six that follow lean on it, and we point out only where each one departs from \Add's pattern.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\(\Add(\pi, D, \mathit{arm}, m)\)}{Add}}\label{sec:add}


\Add{}'s arguments are \(\pi : \mathit{path}\), the node that will receive the new arm as its last child; \(D\), the discipline that node runs; \(\mathit{arm} : \mathit{pol}\), the policy of the new arm; and \(m \in M_{D}\), the metadatum that tells \ensuremath{D} how to schedule that arm (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}).

Say the presently running control is \(C\).
Intuitively, \Add{} inserts a freshly compiled subtree \(\lceil \mathit{arm} \rceil\) as the new last child of the subtree \(C@\pi\).
For traffic to reach the new arm, the flows it serves, those bound for leaves inside it, must be admitted along the whole path.
Every node from the root down to \(C@\pi\) must begin to accept the new flows and send them toward the arm.

\paragraph{Definition}

We must define \(\csem{\Add(\pi, D, \mathit{arm}, m)} : \mathit{control} \rightharpoonup \mathit{control}\) together with the preconditions on the already-running control.

\(\csem{\Add(\pi, D, \mathit{arm}, m)}(C)\) is defined when four things hold: the path \(\pi\) resolves in \(C\) to an internal node, that node runs the discipline \ensuremath{D} the form names, the metadatum \(m\) is drawn from \(M_{D}\), and \ensuremath{\mathit{arm}}'s leaf labels are disjoint from those of \(C\).
The last condition spares \Add{} from relocating traffic.
A change that would move admitted traffic is reached instead as a sequence, in which the old traffic drains in place rather than being re-enqueued (\cref{sec:idioms}).

We define \(\csem{\Add}\) by recursion on \(\pi\), writing \(\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm})\) for the set of flows at \(\lceil \mathit{arm} \rceil\)'s leaves:
{\small
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\csem{\Add([\,],\ D,\ \mathit{arm},\ m)}\ \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}) \;=\\
\quad \langle\mathit{state'},\mathit{pifo},z'\rangle(\mathit{cs}\mathbin{+\!\!+}[\lceil\mathit{arm}\rceil])\\
\quad \textbf{where}\ k=|\mathit{cs}|\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ \mathit{state'}=(\mathit{node\_state},\ \mathit{slot\_states}\mathbin{+\!\!+}[s])\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ s=\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}(\mathit{node\_state},m)\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ z'=\mathtt{setmeta}(\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z},\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm}),k),k,m)\\[8pt]
\csem{\Add(i\mathbin{::}\mathit{rest},\ D,\ \mathit{arm},\ m)}\ \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}) \;=\\
\quad \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},z'\rangle(\mathit{cs}[\,c/i\,])\\
\quad \textbf{where}\ c=\csem{\Add(\mathit{rest},\ D,\ \mathit{arm},\ m)}(\mathit{cs}[i])\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ z'=\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z},\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm}),i)
\end{array}
\]
}
The arguments \ensuremath{D}, \ensuremath{\mathit{arm}}, and \(m\) thread unchanged through the recursion, and \(m\) is consumed only at the base case, which annotates the new slot and seeds its bookkeeping.
Because \(\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm})\) lies outside \(\mathrm{dom}(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves})\), every \(\mathtt{extend}\) along the way is conflict-free.
The recursive case is exactly the \(\mathtt{extend}\) walk of \cref{sec:edit-semantics}, carrying \(\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm})\) down to \(\pi\).

The base case (\(\pi = []\)) has reached the parent of the new arm, where it does three things.
It appends \(\lceil \mathit{arm} \rceil\) as the last child, which lands at the next free slot \(k = |\mathit{cs}|\).
It appends that slot's bookkeeping, \(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}(\mathit{node\_state}, m)\), using the discipline \ensuremath{D} that the form carries.
And it rewrites the node's transaction, extending the classifier to send the new flows to the new child and annotating slot \(k\) with \(m\).
Meanwhile, \ensuremath{\mathit{node\_state}} and \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} are untouched.
Appending the arm is legitimate because our arm-order freedom (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}) lets us place the new child last.
The recursive case steps into the on-path child \(i\), writes the result back, and extends this ancestor's transaction to \(\mathtt{extend}(\mathit{z}, \mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm}), i)\), sending the new flows one step further down toward \(\pi\); its \ensuremath{\mathit{node\_state}}, \ensuremath{\mathit{slot\_states}}, and \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} are carried unchanged.

So every node on the path from the root to \(C@\pi\) has its transaction extended to admit and route the new flows, and nothing else about those nodes changes.
The new flows were admitted nowhere before (by disjointness), so each extension is conflict-free and no in-flight packet's route moves.

\paragraph{Pol-level reading}

We define \(\psem{\cdot}\) by recursion on \(\pi\) as well:
{\small
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\psem{\Add([\,],\ D,\ \mathit{arm},\ m)}\ (D\ \mathit{ts}) \;=\; D(\mathit{ts}\mathbin{+\!\!+}[m{:}\,\mathit{arm}])\\[4pt]
\psem{\Add(i\mathbin{::}\mathit{rest},\ D,\ \mathit{arm},\ m)}\ (E\ \mathit{ts}) \;=\\
\quad E(\mathit{ts}[\,\psem{\Add(\mathit{rest},\ D,\ \mathit{arm},\ m)}(\mathit{ts}[i])/i\,])
\end{array}
\]
}
Its node patterns \(D\ \mathit{ts}\) and \(E\ \mathit{ts}\) split a \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} node into its discipline and child list \(\mathit{ts}\).
The base case reuses the argument name \ensuremath{D}, firing only when the reached node runs that discipline; an ancestor's discipline \(E\) is unconstrained and simply carried along.
The metadatum \(m\) appears on both sides.
\(\csem{\Add}\) records it at \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}(k)\), and \(\psem{\Add}\) writes it onto the new arm, so reading the edited control back recovers the annotation the operator asked for.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\(\ChangeMeta(\tau, D, m)\)}{ChangeMeta}}\label{sec:changemeta}


\ChangeMeta{} re-tunes a single already-running arm, resetting the metadatum its parent schedules it by, without moving a packet or disturbing any sibling.

\(\tau : \mathit{path}\) is the target arm.
The path is non-empty (the target arm is required to have a parent), and we destruct it as \(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k]\), so \(k\) is the arm's slot at its parent \(C@\pi\).
\ensuremath{D} is the discipline the parent runs, and \(m\) is a fresh value from \(M_{D}\) (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}).
Carrying \ensuremath{D} on the form is what makes \(m \in M_{D}\) checkable from the form alone, rather than only against the control it is about to be applied to.

\paragraph{Definition}

\ChangeMeta{} is defined when \(\tau\) resolves in \(C\) to an arm whose parent runs the \ensuremath{D} the form names, when \(m \in M_{D}\), and when that parent's ranker does not name \(\Strictstar\).
Changing the metadata of a \(\Strictstar\) is illegal, because \cref{sec:hw-survivor} gives such a node no \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} of its own, which is sound only while its two priorities stay fixed.
Its node transformer \(\mathtt{setmeta}_k\) rewrites the node's \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} and nothing else:
{\small
\[
\mathtt{setmeta}_k\ \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}) = \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\ \mathtt{setmeta}(\mathit{z},k,m)\rangle(\mathit{cs}).
\]
}
The form applies that transformer at the parent and nowhere else:
\[
\csem{\ChangeMeta(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k],\, D,\, m)} = \mathtt{at}(\pi,\, \mathtt{setmeta}_k).
\]
Under \Strict{} the arm's metadatum is its priority, and under \WFQ{} it is its weight.
That value alone becomes \(m\).
The evolving per-arm bookkeeping beside it, \WFQ's last-finish tag in \(\mathit{slot\_states}[k]\), is left untouched (the Note below says why).

\paragraph{Pol-level reading}

\ChangeMeta{} reannotates arm \(k\) and leaves that arm's policy, its siblings, and the discipline \ensuremath{D} alone:
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\psem{\ChangeMeta(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k],\, D,\, m)} =\\[3pt]
\quad \mathtt{at}\big(\pi,\; D(\ldots, m_k{:}\,t_k, \ldots) \mapsto D(\ldots, m{:}\,t_k, \ldots)\big).
\end{array}
\]

\paragraph{Note: Why the per-arm bookkeeping is preserved}

\ChangeMeta{} rewrites the arm's metadatum and leaves the per-arm bookkeeping beside it untouched.
That is the line \cref{sec:controls} draws between the configuration the operator chose and what serving traffic left behind.
Resetting the bookkeeping would pull the targeted arm out of the round in progress, rewarding or penalizing it for no reason the operator asked for.
Whatever \ensuremath{D} keeps beside the metadatum belongs to \ensuremath{D} (\cref{sec:compilation}), and a re-tuning does not touch it.
A discipline that keeps no per-arm bookkeeping, such as \Strict, does not raise the question.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\(\Quiesce(\tau)\)}{Quiesce}}\label{sec:quiesce}


\(\tau : \mathit{path}\) is the path to the target subtree to silence.

Say the presently running control is \(C\).
\Quiesce{} strikes every flow served under \(C@\tau\) from the admitted sets of the nodes those flows traverse, so that no packet bound for a leaf there is admitted anywhere.
It leaves every node's routing in place, so buffered packets can still be popped out.
This is what keeping \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit}\) separate from \(\mathrm{dom}(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves})\) (\cref{sec:controls}) is for.
A silenced flow leaves the admitted set while its entry in the service map stays, so packets already buffered under the node still have a way out.
This is the only way the containment \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit} \subseteq \mathrm{dom}(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves})\) becomes strict, and only \Remove{} deletes the mapping itself.

\paragraph{Definition}

\(\csem{\Quiesce(\tau)}(C)\) is defined whenever \(\tau\) resolves in \(C\) (the empty path silences the whole tree).
Let \(L = \mathtt{flows}(C@\tau)\) be the flows to silence.
Write \(\mathit{z} \setminus L\) for \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} with \(L\) struck from its admitted set, so \((\mathit{z} \setminus L).\mathit{admit} = \mathit{z}.\mathit{admit} \setminus L\).
Its service map and its ranker are unchanged.
The nodes that those flows traverse are the spine down to \(\tau\) together with \(\tau\)'s whole subtree:
\[
V_\tau = \{\, C@\nu \;:\; \nu \sqsubseteq \tau \,\} \;\cup\; \{\, \text{every node of } C@\tau \,\}.
\] 
\Quiesce{} narrows the admitted set at each of these nodes and touches nothing else, through the node transformer
\[
g_L\ \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}) = \langle\mathit{state},\mathit{pifo},\ \mathit{z} \setminus L\rangle(\mathit{cs}),
\]
applied at every node of \(V_\tau\):
\[
\csem{\Quiesce(\tau)} = \mathtt{at}(V_\tau,\ g_L).
\]
Every field other than \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit}\), and every node outside \(V_\tau\), is left unchanged.

The restriction has to reach every node of \(V_\tau\) rather than the root alone.
Under \cref{sec:controls}'s parallel \push{}, every node on the path from the system root to a packet's destination leaf mints an index via its local \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} independently of the others.
If we restricted \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} at only a subset of those nodes, say only the root or only inside \(C@\tau\), the rest would go on minting indices and enqueueing them.
The tree would then be malformed, and a later \pop{} would get stuck.

\paragraph{Pol-level reading}

\(\psem{\Quiesce(\tau)}\) is the identity on \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\(\Remove(\tau)\)}{Remove}}\label{sec:remove}

As before, \(\tau : \mathit{path}\) is the non-empty target arm; destruct it as \(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k]\).
\Remove{} unhooks that arm and renumbers higher siblings down by one; it fires only when the arm's subtree is empty (see note below).

\paragraph{Definition}

As in \cref{sec:quiesce}, let \(L = \mathtt{flows}(C@\tau)\), here the flows that the doomed arm serves.
Write \(\mathit{z} \dsetminus L\) for \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} with \(L\) struck from its service map, and hence, by the containment of \cref{sec:controls}, from its admitted set too.
Its ranker is unchanged.
\Remove{} drops the arm at its parent and strikes \(L\) from the service map of every proper ancestor above that parent, the set \(A_\pi = \{\, C@\nu \;:\; \nu \sqsubset \pi \,\}\).
It is defined when \(\tau\) resolves in \(C\), when the subtree at \(\tau\) is empty, and when \(C@\tau\)'s ranker does not name \(\Strictstar\).
A \(\Strictstar\) node can only be retired by \Undesignate{} (\cref{sec:undesignate}), which promotes the arm beneath it and is the only way \cref{sec:hw-survivor} leaves the substrate to dismantle one.
Dropping arm \(k\) shifts every later arm one slot to the left, and we name that shift \(\sigma_k\): the map on child indices that fixes every \(i < k\) and sends every \(i > k\) to \(i-1\).
The node transformer \(\mathtt{drop}_k\) unhooks child \(k\) and renumbers what named it:
{\small
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\mathtt{drop}_k\ \langle(\mathit{node\_state}, \mathit{ss}),\ \mathit{pifo},\ \mathit{z}\rangle(\mathit{cs}) \;=\\
\quad \langle(\mathit{node\_state},\ \mathit{ss}[-/k]),\ \sigma_k(\mathit{pifo}),\ z'\rangle(\mathit{cs}[-/k])\\
\quad \textbf{where}\ z'.\mathit{serves} = \sigma_k \circ (\mathit{z} \dsetminus L).\mathit{serves}\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ z'.\mathit{admit} = (\mathit{z} \dsetminus L).\mathit{admit}\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ z'.\mathit{meta} = \mathit{z}.\mathit{meta} \circ \sigma_k^{-1}\\
\quad \phantom{\textbf{where}}\ z'.\mathit{rank} = \mathit{z}.\mathit{rank}
\end{array}
\]
}
The form drops the arm at its parent and retracts \(L\) at every proper ancestor:
\[
\csem{\Remove(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k])} = \mathtt{at}(A_\pi,\ \mathit{z} \dsetminus L) \circ \mathtt{at}(\pi,\, \mathtt{drop}_k).
\]
Emptiness and \(\vdash C\) force \(C@\pi.\mathit{pifo}\) to hold \emph{no entry} equal to \(k\), so \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} loses no entry, and surviving entries keep their child.
\ensuremath{\mathit{node\_state}}, and every field of every node outside \(C@\pi\) and the removed subtree other than the ancestor service maps just named, is unchanged.

\paragraph{Note: Why the ancestors are retracted too}

Dropping the arm at its parent alone would leave every node above still mapping \(L\) toward it.
A later \push{} of an \(L\)-packet would then be admitted at an ancestor and enqueue an index there, while the parent, which no longer serves \(L\), would enqueue nothing.
This would leave \(\vdash\) broken.
\Remove{} retracts unconditionally, so that it is sound on its own even when no \Quiesce{} has run, as when it undoes an \Add{} whose arm never carried traffic.

\paragraph{Pol-level reading}

\Remove{} drops arm \(k\) from the parent \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} node:
\[
\psem{\Remove(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k])} = \mathtt{at}(\pi,\; D(\mathit{ts}) \mapsto D(\mathit{ts}[-/k])).
\]

\paragraph{Note: Why insist on an empty subtree}

Deleting an \emph{occupied} child has no canonical local realization.
Consider the well-formed tree of \cref{fig:remove-orig}, whose internal nodes are \(P\) and \(Q\), with the annotations on the arms showing how a parent refers to a child.
Say the operator asks us to delete \tritext{flowC}, dropping its packets.
To restore well-formedness, we need to remove two instances of the index \(1\) from \(Q\)'s PIFO and two instances of the index \(0\) from \(P\)'s PIFO.

At \(Q\) the edit is unambiguous.
Well-formedness of the original tree forces \(Q\)'s PIFO to have exactly two instances of \(1\), and we delete those two.
The trouble is one level up.
\(P\)'s PIFO originally had four instances of \(0\), and well-formedness demands that we remove two of them.
But \emph{which} two?
No entry in \(P\) carries the meta-information ``I was enqueued when a packet was inserted into \tritext{flowC}''; an entry \(0\) in \(P\) only means ``when this index is popped, recursively ask subtree \(Q\) to emit the next packet'' (see \cref{sec:hierarchical}).
This absence of meta-information is a \emph{feature} of PIFO trees, not a defect: an entry is deliberately detached from the packet it was enqueued with, which is what lets each node schedule its own discipline in isolation and lets a subtree be reconfigured without rewriting its ancestors.

Given this, there is no obvious or operator-provided answer: whichever two instances of \(0\) we strike, the choice is itself a scheduling decision, since it affects how \(P\) intermixes \tritext{flowB} traffic with \(Q\)'s.
Strike the two rightmost \(0\)s (\cref{fig:remove-strike-right}) and five pops drain \(P\) as \popseq{flowB,flowB,flowA,flowB,flowA}.
Strike the two leftmost instead (\cref{fig:remove-strike-left}) and the same five pops drain it as \popseq{flowA,flowB,flowA,flowB,flowB}.
The \emph{choice of which} \(0\)s to strike \emph{silently reschedules \tritext{flowA} against \tritext{flowB}}, and nothing in the operator's deletion request records what the operator wanted.

We do not wish to make a scheduling decision for the operator, and the only way to avoid one is to drain the whole tree, filter out \tritext{flowC}'s packets, and re-push, which is a stop-the-world rebuild.

Our grammar sidesteps the question entirely, by asking that a targeted subtree be empty before it is deleted (\cref{sec:sequences}).
At the instant \Remove{} fires, no entry above the doomed arm was enqueued on account of a packet inside it, so no entry has to be dropped and the structural deletion is unambiguous.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\(\Designate(\tau, \mathit{survivor})\)}{Designate}}\label{sec:designate}


\(\tau : \mathit{path}\) is the path to the target subtree being wrapped, and \(\mathit{survivor} : \mathit{pol}\) is the designated-survivor policy that becomes the new \(\Strictstar\) node's low-priority arm.

Say the presently running control is \(C\).
Intuitively, \Designate{} replaces \(C@\tau\) in place with \(\Strictstar(C@\tau, \lceil \mathit{survivor} \rceil)\).
It is the mechanism behind non-disruptive in-place replacement (\cref{fig:designate}).
The \(\Strictstar\) node lets \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} begin accepting new traffic on a low-priority arm, while the old subtree keeps draining, untouched, on a high-priority one.
Once the old subtree is empty, \Undesignate{} (\cref{sec:undesignate}) removes it and promotes \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} in its place.
That is why the form exists: no single edit can swap one subtree for another without either dropping the old subtree's backlog or stalling the new arm, but the \(\Strictstar\) node lets both coexist for the length of a drain.

\Designate{} installs a ranker that names \(\Strictstar\), and \Undesignate{} retires the node carrying it.
A node's ranker records which discipline the node runs (\cref{sec:controls}), so both forms state their preconditions structurally.
No other form disturbs such a node.
\Remove{} will not retire one (\cref{sec:remove}), and \ChangeMeta{} will not retune its arms (\cref{sec:changemeta}), so its two priorities are the ones \Designate{} gave it for as long as it exists.
That node is visible at \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level as a \(\Strictstar\), which is what \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) (\cref{sec:equivalences}) reports of it.
An operator may write a \(\Strictstar\) directly (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}), but one usually arises in the middle of a planner sequence, between a \Designate{} and its eventual \Undesignate.
\cref{sec:hardware} takes up why the two disciplines are kept apart, which turns out to be a question about hardware layout rather than about scheduling.

\paragraph{Definition}

\(\csem{\Designate(\tau, \mathit{survivor})}(C)\) is defined when \(\tau\) resolves in \(C\), and when every flow \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} serves is one that either nothing in \(C\) serves or that \(C@\tau\) itself serves.
So \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} may reuse the flows of the subtree it is replacing, and it may bring flows that are new to the whole tree, but it may not claim a flow that some other part of \(C\) is already serving.
Reuse is the interesting case and the Note below takes it up.
\cref{fig:designate} draws the target subtree as \tritext{oldcol} and the survivor as \tritext{newcol}, and below we call them the old and new arms.
Write \(M = C@\tau\) for the target, and let \(P_0\) be the number of packets buffered under it (\(P_0 = 3\) in \cref{fig:designate-before}).
\Designate{} wraps the target and admits \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}'s flows at every ancestor on the way down.
That walk is \Add's, with \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}'s flows in place of the new arm's.
The two differ only at the target, since \Add{} appends an arm there while \Designate{} wraps the node it has reached.
The transformer \(\mathtt{wrap}\) replaces \(M\) with a fresh \(\Strictstar\) node \(N\) over the two arms \([\,M,\ \lceil\mathit{survivor}\rceil\,]\), giving the old arm strict priority over the new arm:
{\small
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\mathtt{wrap}(M) = N = \langle s_N,\ q_N,\ \mathit{z}_N \rangle\big([\,M,\ \lceil\mathit{survivor}\rceil\,]\big),\ \textbf{where}\\[3pt]
\quad n_N = \mathit{init\_node\_state}_{\Strictstar},\\[3pt]
\quad s_N = (\,n_N,\ [\,\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{\Strictstar}(n_N, \mathtt{hi}),\\
\quad\qquad\qquad\ \ \mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{\Strictstar}(n_N, \mathtt{lo})\,]\,),\\[3pt]
\quad q_N = \text{the \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} of } P_0 \text{ copies of the entry } (0,\ \mathtt{hi}),\\[3pt]
\quad \mathit{z}_N.\mathit{meta} = \{\, 0 \mapsto \mathtt{hi},\ 1 \mapsto \mathtt{lo} \,\},\\[3pt]
\quad \mathit{z}_N(p) = (1,\ \mathtt{lo})\ \text{if}\ \mathit{flow}(p) \in \mathtt{flows}(\mathit{survivor}),\ \text{else}\ (0,\ \mathtt{hi}).
\end{array}
\]
}
In the vocabulary of \cref{sec:controls}, \(\mathit{z}_N\) serves \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}'s flows at arm \(1\) and everything else the node serves at arm \(0\), and admits both.
Its ranker names \(\Strictstar\) rather than \Strict, which is what \cref{sec:undesignate}'s \Undesignate{} later looks for.
Arm \(0\) is \(M\) (\textsf{old}), unchanged down to its whole subtree and buffered packets; arm \(1\) is the freshly compiled \(\lceil\mathit{survivor}\rceil\) (\textsf{new}), initially empty.
This is \cref{fig:designate-after}.
\(N\)'s index-\ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} holds \(P_0\) entries for arm \(0\) and none for arm \(1\), one per packet buffered under \textsf{old}.
That backlog therefore keeps popping through \(N\) ahead of any later arrival, unchanged, while \textsf{new} fills only as fresh traffic arrives (\cref{fig:designate-drain}).
Because \(\mathtt{at}\) writes \(N\) back into the parent's slot \(k\), the parent's \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}(k)\) is unchanged, so \(N\) is transparent to the parent.
If \(\tau\) is empty the whole control becomes \(N\), with the old root as arm \(0\).

Like \(\csem{\Add}\), the form is a recursion on the path, and only its base case is new:
\[
\csem{\Designate([\,],\ \mathit{survivor})}\ M = \mathtt{wrap}(M).
\]
Its recursive case is \(\csem{\Add}\)'s, carrying \(\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{survivor})\) rather than \(\mathtt{flows}(\mathit{arm})\) down toward \(\tau\).

\paragraph{Note: Overlap between the old subtree and \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}}

Overlap of leaf labels between \(C@\tau\) and \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} is explicitly permitted.
The moment when \(\delta\) fires becomes the boundary between old traffic and new.
Old traffic continues to drain from arm 0, which is \(C@\tau\) carried over; new traffic goes to arm 1.
From that instant on, an arriving packet whose label appears in \(\lceil \mathit{survivor} \rceil\) is sent by \(N.\mathit{z}\) to slot 1, while the residual packets already under arm 0 drain on their existing \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} entries.
The two arms thus stay operationally disjoint even when their label sets overlap.

\paragraph{Pol-level reading}

\Designate{} wraps the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} subtree at \(\tau\):
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\psem{\Designate(\tau, \mathit{survivor})} ={}\\
\quad \mathtt{at}(\tau, t \mapsto \Strictstar(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,t, \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\mathit{survivor})).
\end{array}
\]

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\(\Undesignate(\tau)\)}{Undesignate}}\label{sec:undesignate}


\(\tau : \mathit{path}\) targets the \(\Strictstar\) node to unwrap.
\Undesignate{} is the structural inverse of \Designate.
It drops a \(\Strictstar\) node whose arm 0 is empty, leaving arm 1 behind (the last step of \cref{fig:designate}).
Below we write \(N\) for that node and \(B\) for its surviving arm, both as controls, and \(|B|\) for the number of packets buffered under \(B\).

\paragraph{Definition}

\Undesignate{} is defined when \(C@\tau\)'s ranker names \(\Strictstar\), and when its arm 0 is empty.
\(\mathtt{unwrap}\) returns arm 1 of the node, \(B\), with its subtree and contents unchanged; the node \(N = C@\tau\) and its empty arm-0 subtree are discarded.
Since \(\mathtt{at}\) writes \(B\) back into the parent's slot \(k\), the parent's \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}(k)\) is unchanged and \(B\) now hangs directly where \(N\) did:
\[
\csem{\Undesignate(\tau)} = \mathtt{at}(\tau,\, \mathtt{unwrap}).
\]

\paragraph{Pol-level reading}

\Undesignate{} collapses the wrapper and leaves its low arm behind:
\[
\psem{\Undesignate(\tau)} = \mathtt{at}(\tau,\; \Strictstar(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,a,\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,b) \mapsto b).
\]
That arm is \(\lfloor B \rfloor\).
The wrapper's own annotations go with it, and the surviving arm inherits the annotation the wrapper held at its parent.
The empty-arm-0 precondition is operational and leaves no trace at \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level.

\section{Correctness}\label{sec:correctness}

\cref{sec:edit-semantics} gave each form of \(\delta\) two readings, and two things have to be true of them.
The first is that they agree, so that the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} we echo to the operator is the one the live control realizes.
The second is that installing an edit costs the buffered backlog nothing.
This section states both as theorems and proves them one form at a time.

\subsection{Agreement}\label{sec:interplay}

The situation we care about is a single \(\delta\) firing on a running control.
Consider the following commuting square, which we will read with an eye to the operator's experience.
Our final goal will be to correctly relate \(C_2'\) and \(C_2\).

The operator writes \(p_1\), which is compiled into control \(C_1 := \lceil p_1 \rceil\).
Recall that \(\lfloor C_1 \rfloor \eqR p_1\) by compiler correctness (\cref{sec:equivalences}).
We echo \(p_1' := \lfloor C_1 \rfloor\) back to the operator.
\(p_1'\) matters because it faithfully reports the arm ordering that the compiler chose.
Push and pop operations then carry \(C_1\) to \(C_1'\).
We know that \(C_1 \sim C_1'\), since \push{} and \pop{} changed only live contents, and \(\lfloor C_1' \rfloor = \lfloor C_1 \rfloor\) by \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\)'s insensitivity to live contents, so the live \(C_1'\) still realizes both \(p_1\) and \(p_1'\).

The operator next writes \(p_2\), and the \emph{transition planner} (\cref{sec:planner}), the tool that infers atomic edits between two \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}s, produces a \(\delta\) such that \(\psem{\delta}(p_1') \eqR p_2\).
Applying \(\csem{\delta}\) to \(C_1'\) brings us to control \(C_2'\).
By closure under \(\delta\) (\cref{thm:interplay} below) we have \(\lfloor C_2' \rfloor \eqR \psem{\delta}(p_1')\).
Chaining this with the previously established \(\psem{\delta}(p_1') \eqR p_2\) gives \(\lfloor C_2' \rfloor \eqR p_2\).
We again echo \(p_2' := \lfloor C_2' \rfloor\) to the operator.

The transformation is complete at this point, but it still needs a reference point.
\(C_2 := \lceil p_2 \rceil\) is the control that the state of the art would have built, since all it can do is compile a new control from scratch.
\emph{We} do not actually construct \(C_2\), but it is still valuable to relate \(C_2'\), which we have just produced, to \(C_2\).

By compiler correctness, \(\lfloor C_2 \rfloor \eqR p_2\), hence \(\lfloor C_2' \rfloor \eqR \lfloor C_2 \rfloor\).
However, we would like to relate the \emph{controls} directly, not just relate their \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level projections via \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\).
The control-level relation we can write is \(C_2' \simR C_2\), which absorbs two gaps at once.
\(C_2'\) carries the live \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} and \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} accumulated since \(C_1\), while \(C_2\) is freshly compiled and bare; the \(\sim\) component covers that.
\(\csem{\delta}\) and \(\lceil \cdot \rceil\) are also free to pick different sibling orders at internal nodes, so the two may differ in child arrangements; the R-closure covers that.

\begin{theorem}[Interplay of \(\psem{\delta}\) and \(\csem{\delta}\)]\label{thm:interplay}
Let \(C\) be a well-formed control, and let \(\csem{\delta}(C)\) be defined for some form \(\delta\) of the grammar (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}).
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\csem{\delta}(C)\) is well-formed.
\item \(\lfloor \csem{\delta}(C) \rfloor \eqR \psem{\delta}(\lfloor C \rfloor)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each clause aggregates one of the per-form checks of \cref{sec:per-form}.
Clause 1 is exactly \emph{Preservation of \(\vdash\)}, and clause 2 is exactly \emph{Characterization}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Note: Why clause 2 is not vacuous}

Clause 2 can look true by construction, as though \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) were being defined to make it hold.
It is not.
\(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) is fixed in advance, by structural recursion on the control (\cref{sec:equivalences}), rather than extended along \(\delta\).
So each form's Characterization has to show that this fixed recursion, rerun on the edited control, lands where \(\psem{\delta}\) independently says it should.

The diagram above is the unit cell of a fuller picture: a real reconfiguration is a \emph{sequence} of edits, not one \(\delta\), and \cref{sec:idioms} lines up copies of this cell end to end, each intermediate control carrying its own \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) meaning.

\paragraph{A worked example}

The operator requests \(p_1 = \Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\).
The compiler uses its degree of freedom to yield control \(C_1\) such that \(\lfloor C_1 \rfloor = \Strict(\mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom})\); the children have been reordered for some compiler-internal reason.
We echo back
\[
p_1' := \lfloor C_1 \rfloor = \Strict(\mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom}).
\]
We know that \(p_1 \eqR p_1'\), so the scheduler that the operator gets is still the one they asked for.
As the control serves pushes and pops, \(C_1\) transforms into \(C_1'\): the same tree with its \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}s and \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}s advanced, so it is still the case that \(C_1 \sim C_1'\) and \(\lfloor C_1' \rfloor = p_1'\).

Later, the operator requests \(p_2 = \Strict(\mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{mid}{:}\,\textsf{spotify},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom})\).
The runtime can again use its freedom: instead of literally splicing \textsf{spotify} in between running arms \textsf{gmail} and \textsf{zoom}, it chooses to append \textsf{spotify} to the end.
This converts the running control \(C_1'\) into control \(C_2'\) such that
\[
\lfloor C_2' \rfloor = \Strict(\mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\allowbreak\ \mathtt{mid}{:}\,\textsf{spotify}),
\]
and we echo \(p_2' := \lfloor C_2' \rfloor\) back to the operator.

This is \cref{thm:interplay} on a small scale.
The operator wrote \(p_1\) then \(p_2\), the runtime exercised its arm-order freedom to produce \(C_1'\) then \(C_2'\), and at each step we read off and echoed the running representative, \(p_1'\) and then \(p_2'\).
Since \(\lfloor C_2' \rfloor \eqR p_2\), the operator's request is faithfully realized despite the different slot order.

\subsection{Non-disruption}\label{sec:nondisruption}

Agreement on its own is not enough.
The stop-the-world reset of \cref{sec:intro} is realizable and explainable too, and it is the baseline we wish to beat.
We beat it with the property of \emph{non-disruption}.
Installing \(\delta\) may disturb previously buffered traffic \emph{only} in service of the newly installed policy.
The \emph{very act of changing policies} never introduces arbitrary artifacts such as drops, reorders, or rewrites among buffered packets.
Each buffered entry keeps the rank it was given when it was \push{}ed, so nothing buffered is lost, corrupted, or reordered relative to the rest of the backlog.

Ranks are frozen at \push{} time, and that cuts both ways.
Inverting the two priorities of \(\Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,A,\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,B)\) with \ChangeMeta{} does \emph{not} change the next \pop{}, since the parent's index-\ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} already holds \(A\) at \(\mathtt{hi}\) and \(B\) at \(\mathtt{lo}\) and \ChangeMeta{} rewrites neither.
Only packets pushed after the change are ranked under the new priorities, so a \(B\)-packet arriving after the inversion is served ahead of one that arrived before it.
That is the reconfigured policy \emph{correctly} governing new arrivals, not a disturbance of the backlog.

Here is why I want to keep it.
Suppose we added a form \(\textsf{Reset}(p_2)\), with \(\csem{\textsf{Reset}(p_2)}(C) = \lceil p_2 \rceil\) and \(\psem{\textsf{Reset}(p_2)}(p) = p_2\).
It satisfies both clauses of \cref{thm:interplay}, since \(\lceil p_2 \rceil\) is well-formed and \(\lfloor \lceil p_2 \rceil \rfloor \eqR p_2\), and it is exactly the stop-the-world baseline: buffered packets dropped on the floor.

We state the property on what the network can observe, which is the sequence of packets that successive \pop{}s release.

\begin{theorem}[Non-disruption]\label{thm:nondisruption}
Let \(C\) be a well-formed control, and let \(\csem{\delta}(C)\) be defined for some form \(\delta\) of the grammar (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}).
Then \(C\) and \(\csem{\delta}(C)\) release the same sequence of packets under any run of \pop{}s alone.
\end{theorem}

Most forms discharge this through a single fact about \pop{}, and need no argument of their own.

\begin{lemma}[Non-disruption reduces to \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}-content preservation]\label{lem:nondisruption}
A \pop{} reads only \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} contents: it pops the root index-\ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} to a child, recurses, and finally emits a leaf packet, never consulting any \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} or \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} (\cref{sec:controls}).
Write \(C' = \csem{\delta}(C)\), and suppose \(\csem{\delta}\) leaves the contents of every non-empty \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} of \(C\) unchanged (never rewriting, reordering, or removing a live entry, and introducing only empty \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}s).
Then the buffered backlog is preserved entry-for-entry in \(C'\) and \(\delta\) is non-disruptive.
\end{lemma}

We therefore discharge non-disruption per form by naming the form's \emph{footprint}, the nodes and fields it touches, and checking that the footprint holds no live \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} entry.
Four of the six pass that check outright.
\Designate{} inserts a node whose \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} is not empty and \Undesignate{} drops one, so those two need an argument of their own.
Each such argument shows that the \pop{} sequence is unchanged, which is why \cref{thm:nondisruption} is stated on that sequence rather than on \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} contents.

\subsection{Form by Form}\label{sec:per-form}

Each form owes three checks, and together the six sets of three prove the two theorems above.
Two of the checks feed \cref{thm:interplay}, and the third feeds \cref{thm:nondisruption}.
Throughout, we write \(C\) for the pre-edit control and \(C'\) for the post-edit control, and \(p = \lfloor C \rfloor\), \(p' = \lfloor C' \rfloor\) for the policies they realize.
All three checks assume \(\csem{\delta}(C)\) is defined.
Outside the region \cref{sec:edit-semantics} specifies, \(\csem{\delta}\) is undefined.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Preservation of \(\vdash\).} \(\vdash C\) implies \(\vdash C'\).
Any packets or index entries that \(\csem{\delta}\) drops or adds must leave the per-node pifo and packet counts in balance.
\item \emph{Characterization.} Prove that reading the policy off the edited control agrees, up to \(\eqR\), with applying the \(\psem{\delta}\) of \cref{sec:edit-semantics} to the policy read off the original: \(p' \eqR \psem{\delta}(p)\).
\item \emph{Non-disruption.} Name the form's footprint and check that it holds no live \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} entry, so that \cref{lem:nondisruption} applies.
Where it does not apply, argue directly that the \pop{} sequence is unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\cref{fig:per-form-checks} discharges all eighteen checks.
Fifteen of them are immediate from the rules of \cref{sec:edit-semantics} and are given in the table itself.
The remaining three are the ones with an argument to make, and the rest of this section gives those: \Add's Characterization, and non-disruption for \Designate{} and \Undesignate.

\subsubsection{Characterization for \texorpdfstring{\Add}{Add}}\label{sec:add-char}

Both recursions descend \(\pi\) in the same shape, so we induct on \(\pi\).
At the base (\(\pi = []\)), \(\psem{\cdot}\) appends the annotated arm \(m{:}\,\mathit{arm}\), while \(\csem{\Add}\) appends \(\lceil \mathit{arm} \rceil\) at slot \(k\) and sets \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}(k)\) to \(m\).
We have \(\lfloor \lceil \mathit{arm} \rceil \rfloor \eqR \mathit{arm}\), and \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) reads the annotation back off \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\).
So the two agree on the new arm up to \(\eqR\), and every existing arm is untouched.
At a recursive step, both descend into child \(i\) and write the result back, so the claim follows from the induction hypothesis.
The ancestor itself adds nothing to reconcile.
\(\csem{\Add}\) leaves its \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} and \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} unchanged, and its \ensuremath{\mathit{z}}-extension is confined to the classifier of an internal node, which \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) does not read.
So the two sides agree all the way down the spine and can differ only inside \(\lceil \mathit{arm} \rceil\), where sibling order is free; that is exactly the \(\eqR\)-slack the claim allows, giving \(p' \eqR \psem{\Add(\pi, D, \mathit{arm}, m)}(p)\).

\subsubsection{Non-disruption for \texorpdfstring{\Designate{} and \Undesignate}{Designate and Undesignate}}\label{sec:wrap-nondisruption}

These are the two forms \cref{lem:nondisruption} does not reach.
\(\mathtt{wrap}\) inserts the live node \(N\) with a non-empty \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}, and \(\mathtt{unwrap}\) drops one, so each needs a direct argument that the \pop{} sequence is unchanged.
As in \cref{sec:undesignate}, \(N\) is the wrapper and \(B\) the control under its arm 1.

For \Designate, every in-flight packet lies under arm 0, which is \(C@\tau\) carried over unchanged, so its own entries are untouched.
If \(P_0 > 0\), every entry in \(N.\mathit{pifo}\) is slot \(0\), so any \pop{} reaching \(N\) descends into arm 0 and returns exactly what it would have returned before.
If \(P_0 = 0\), then \(\vdash C\) means no \pop{} ever reaches \(N\).
Arm 1 sees traffic only on a future \push{}, so no buffered packet is dropped, reordered, or re-ranked.

For \Undesignate, the only entries dropped are \(N\)'s slot-\(1\) entries, and with arm 0 empty every \pop{} reaching \(N\) was already forced to slot \(1\) and on to \(B\).
Eliding \(N\) removes that redundant hop and returns the same packet, and no entry inside \(B\) moves.


\paragraph{Taking stock}

With \cref{fig:per-form-checks}, that is all six forms, and together they discharge \cref{thm:interplay} and \cref{thm:nondisruption}.
The Characterizations have content because each was proved against a \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) fixed in advance, so they are six separate claims rather than six readings of one definition.

\paragraph{Note: The check that is deliberately absent}

A fourth check might be expected, and is not there.
Nothing has to be proved about the \ensuremath{\mathit{state}} of the nodes an edit leaves alone, because every \(\csem{\delta}\) rule names each field of the node it produces, and \(\mathtt{at}(\pi, g)\) copies every off-path node by definition.
What state survives an edit is therefore read off \cref{sec:edit-semantics} rather than argued for.

\subsection{Preserving this proof down to hardware}\label{sec:hw-preservation}

\cref{sec:per-form} proves soundness at the \(\delta\) level, where each form (\Add, \Quiesce, \Remove, \ldots) carries a well-formed tree to a well-formed tree.
To run on hardware, each edit is lowered, by a simple and mechanical compilation, into a sequence of fine-grained instructions drawn from the substrate's instruction set.
That instruction set and the substrate machinery are the subject of \cref{sec:hardware}.
Here we need only two things about those instructions.
They are fine-grained, each doing one small thing such as allocating a node, attaching a node to a parent, or admitting a flow at a node.
A single such instruction, unlike a whole form of \(\delta\), \emph{can} leave the tree malformed.

We do not prove soundness at the instruction level, but instead informally make the case for why the \cref{sec:per-form} proof survives the lowering.
There are two reasons.
First, the compilation is \emph{faithful}: each form of \(\delta\) expands to a fixed instruction sequence that, when run to completion, realizes exactly that form's effect.
We give the translation from each \(\delta\) form to its instruction sequence and take its faithfulness to be uncontroversial.
Second, our substrate runs each such sequence as a single \emph{commit}: a group of instructions that installs as one instant, with no \push{} or \pop{} interleaved between them (\cref{sec:hw-commit} spells out the substrate machinery that makes this so).
The transiently-malformed intermediate trees are therefore never observed by the network.
That commit is precisely how the substrate \emph{realizes} the atomicity property of \cref{sec:grammar}.
Atomicity asked for an instantaneous control replacement between two \push{}/\pop{} operations, and the commit collapses a multi-instruction lowering into one such instant.
Every \push{}/\pop{} therefore still lands on a well-formed control (\(p_1\), some transitionary policy, or \(p_2\)), exactly as \cref{sec:per-form} proved.

The same argument carries from the instruction sequence down to hardware.
The hardware executes a committed sequence atomically with respect to \push{} and \pop{}, so what it exhibits is again what \cref{sec:per-form} proved.
The compilation itself, and the substrate machinery that makes a commit atomic, are the subject of \cref{sec:hardware}.

\section{Realizing Reconfigurations as Guarded Sequences}\label{sec:sequences}

This section composes the forms of \(\delta\) into \emph{guarded sequences} \((\varphi ; \delta)^{*}\), where a guard \(\varphi\) is a predicate on the state of the live control.
Guarded sequences realize changes to the live control that no single \(\delta\) can express.
We first show that the transitionary period such a sequence passes through needs no new semantics (\cref{sec:links}), then give the grammar of guarded sequences (\cref{sec:idioms}).

\subsection{Transitionary Policies and their Semantics}\label{sec:links}

A guarded sequence threads the live control through a chain of intermediate controls.
We call each such intermediate control a \emph{transitionary policy}, and we write \(\mathit{link}_i\) for the control on which the \(i\)-th \(\delta\) fires, counting from zero.
So \(\mathit{link}_0 = C\) is the starting control, and \(\mathit{link}_{i+1} = \csem{\delta_i}(\mathit{link}_i)\) is what the \(i\)-th \(\delta\) leaves behind.

Each transitionary policy is itself an ordinary \cref{sec:controls}-style control, so the transitionary period \emph{needs no new semantics}.
That is the promise of \cref{sec:intro}, discharged locally by the per-form checks of \cref{sec:per-form}.
Moreover, since every form of \cref{sec:grammar} is \emph{\ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-explainable} (each \(\delta\) has a \(\psem{\delta}\) that tracks its \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level effect), we can echo to the operator, at every step of the sequence, the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} that the live control actually realizes.
The chain of \(\psem{\cdot}\)s starting from \(\lfloor C \rfloor\) gives \(\lfloor \mathit{link}_i \rfloor\) for each \(i\), so the operator always knows which policy is running.

\paragraph{Follow-up requests}
The transition planner engages with one reconfiguration at a time.
Suppose the operator submits a follow-up \(p_3\) while a \(p_1 \to p_2\) sequence is still in flight, some guard \(\varphi_i\) not yet having become true.
We queue \(p_3\), and the planner does not begin work on it until the in-flight sequence completes and the live control \(C_z\) satisfies \(\lfloor C_z \rfloor \eqR p_2\).
At that instant the planner pulls \(p_3\) from the queue, treats \(\lfloor C_z \rfloor\) as the new starting point, and produces a fresh sequence to reach \(p_3\).
A more aggressive strategy is possible: splicing or canceling the in-flight sequence to converge on \(p_3\) directly, perhaps at lower total cost than running \(p_1 \to p_2 \to p_3\) in series.
We do not pursue this here, since the splicing analysis introduces its own correctness obligations: the atomicity of the splice, and what transitionary policy the operator observes between the splice and \(p_3\).

\subsection{Guarded Sequences and their Idioms}\label{sec:idioms}

\paragraph{Grammar}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
\textbf{Guarded sequences}\\[3pt]
\varphi \mathrel{::=} \mathtt{true} \;\mid\; \mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path})\\[2pt]
\mathit{gseq} \mathrel{::=} \epsilon \;\mid\; (\varphi\,;\, \delta)\,;\, \mathit{gseq}\\[8pt]
\textbf{Idioms}\\[3pt]
\Retire(\mathit{path}) \;=\;\\
\quad (\mathtt{true};\, \Quiesce(\mathit{path}))\,;\\
\quad (\mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path});\, \Remove(\mathit{path}))\\[4pt]
\SlowRetire(\mathit{path}) \;=\; (\mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path});\, \Remove(\mathit{path}))\\[4pt]
\Replace(\mathit{path}, \mathit{survivor}) \;=\;\\
\quad (\mathtt{true};\, \Designate(\mathit{path}, \mathit{survivor}))\,;\\
\quad (\mathtt{true};\, \Quiesce(\mathit{path} \mathbin{+\!\!+} [0]))\,;\\
\quad (\mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path} \mathbin{+\!\!+} [0]);\, \Undesignate(\mathit{path}))
\end{array}
\]
\caption{The grammar of guarded sequences and the three starter idioms, each a named \((\varphi;\delta)^{*}\).}
\label{fig:gseq-idioms}
\end{figure}

We write \(\mathit{gseq}\) for a guarded sequence (\cref{fig:gseq-idioms}, top).
A guard \(\varphi\) is either trivially satisfied (\(\mathtt{true}\)) or asserts that the subtree at \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} is empty; it is always paired with exactly one \(\delta\).
Those two guard forms have sufficed for every sequence we have needed.
The framework does not depend on this minimality, and a richer predicate language is welcome if desired.

\paragraph{Intuition}

The hardware substrate (\cref{sec:hardware}) does two things beyond ordinary \push{}/\pop{}: it waits for a guard \(\varphi\) to become true on the live control, and it fires a single \(\delta\) as an atomic replacement.
A guarded sequence chains these: wait for \(\varphi_0\), fire \(\delta_0\), then wait for \(\varphi_1\) (now evaluated on the post-\(\delta_0\) control), fire \(\delta_1\), and so on.
Each intermediate control between two consecutive \(\delta\) firings is well-formed, and we call this property the sequence's \emph{safety}.
Safety is entirely local, following from the per-form checks of \cref{sec:per-form}.

\paragraph{Reaching the target}

Safety guarantees that every intermediate control is well-formed, but not that the sequence \emph{arrives} at the intended \(p_2\).
We check arrival at the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} level, folding each form's \(\psem{\delta_i}\) along the sequence.
Write \(\mathit{ip}_i\) for the \(i\)-th intermediate \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}.
Starting from \(\mathit{ip}_0 = \lfloor C \rfloor\), the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} we echoed to the operator, set \(\mathit{ip}_{i+1} := \psem{\delta_i}(\mathit{ip}_i)\).
The sequence is admissible when every \(\psem{\delta_i}\) is defined on \(\mathit{ip}_i\) and the final \(\mathit{ip}_{n+1} \eqR p_2\).
This is the Interplay theorem (\cref{sec:interplay}) iterated across the sequence.
Each \(\psem{\delta_i}\) matches its operational counterpart \(\csem{\delta_i}\) (\cref{sec:per-form}), so a fold that lands at \(\mathit{ip}_{n+1} \eqR p_2\) certifies, cell by cell, that the operational chain ends in a control whose \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) is \(p_2\).
Guards play no role in this check: they govern \emph{when} each \(\delta_i\) fires on the live control and are \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-invisible (\cref{sec:edit-semantics}), so a \((\mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path}); \Remove(\mathit{path}))\) step folds exactly as if its guard were \(\mathtt{true}\).
If the chain fails to reach \(p_2\), or some \(\psem{\delta_i}\) is undefined on its \(\mathit{ip}_i\), the sequence is rejected.
The rejection identifies the offending step, either the first \(\delta_i\) whose \(\psem{\delta_i}\) was undefined or else the final mismatch \(\mathit{ip}_{n+1} \not\eqR p_2\).
Both clients of the framework rely on this check: the planner (\cref{sec:planner}) uses it as a backstop on the sequences it assembles, and an operator authoring by hand (\cref{sec:imperative}) uses it to validate theirs.

\paragraph{Liveness}

Safety is unconditional; liveness is not, and cannot be.
A guarded sequence progresses only as its guards become true, and a guard like \(\mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path})\) becomes true only once the traffic under \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} drains.
That is up to the network, not up to us.
A persistent or adversarial flow can keep a subtree busy indefinitely, and then any step waiting on that subtree's emptiness never fires.
This is inherent.
Draining a subtree gracefully means letting its buffered packets leave on their own, and the only way to speed that up is to drop them, which the grammar refuses to do.
So completing a sequence takes the operator's cooperation, for instance quiescing a flow at its source or accepting a harder reconfiguration that resets more of the tree but drops nothing.
The live control stays safe throughout, but may not reach the target \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} until the traffic allows.
Follow-up requests submitted during a stalled sequence are queued (\cref{sec:links}).

\paragraph{Idioms}

A sequence that reconfigures a real tree is a long list of small edits, and such a list does not tell the operator what is being done to their scheduler.
\emph{Idioms} are a named vocabulary for the sequences that recur, each one collecting a run of edits into a single goal acting on a single subtree.
The transition planner (\cref{sec:planner}) emits them, so what the operator reads back is ``retire this subtree'' rather than a silencing, a wait, and an unhooking.

An idiom is a macro over \(\delta\), and recursively over other idioms, that expands into a fixed \ensuremath{\mathit{gseq}}.
Expansion changes nothing about soundness, since the checks above run on the expanded sequence exactly as they would on a hand-written one.
So an expansion that would reach an undefined form on the current control is rejected.

We name three starter idioms, with their expansions collected in \cref{fig:gseq-idioms}.
New ones can be added later without changing the framework, since an idiom is only a named \ensuremath{\mathit{gseq}}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\Retire(\ensuremath{\mathit{path}})} quiesces the subtree at \ensuremath{\mathit{path}}, waits for it to drain to empty, then \Remove{}s it.
This is the operator-facing way to say ``tear this subtree down gracefully.''

\item \textbf{\SlowRetire(\ensuremath{\mathit{path}})} waits for the subtree at \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} to drain, then \Remove{}s it.
This suits a subtree that must keep receiving traffic while it is being phased out.

\item \textbf{\Replace(\ensuremath{\mathit{path}}, \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}})} phases \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} in and \(\mathit{pol}@\mathit{path}\) out.
It \Designate{}s \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}, quiesces the original subtree, waits for it to drain to empty, and then removes that subtree and the \(\Strictstar\) wrapper, leaving \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}} alone at \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} (\cref{fig:gseq-idioms}).
At the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} level the effect is the outright replacement of \(\mathit{pol}@\mathit{path}\) by \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}.

\end{itemize}

\cref{sec:intro}'s transition supplies a worked example, with the scheduler running \(p_1\) while its operator asks for \(p_2\), where
{\small
\[
\begin{array}{@{}r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{}}
p_1 &=& \Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\textsf{gmail})\\[2pt]
p_2 &=& \Strict(\mathtt{hi}{:}\,\textsf{zoom},\ \mathtt{lo}{:}\,\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify})).
\end{array}
\]}
\Add{} cannot directly reach \(p_2\), since the \textsf{gmail} leaf has to become an interior \RR{} node and \Add{} only appends arms to a node that is already internal.
\Replace{} at the \(\mathtt{lo}\) slot does reach it, with survivor \(\RR(\textsf{gmail},\allowbreak\ \textsf{spotify})\).
This is the transition \cref{sec:intro} reaches at \(t_2\).

\section{The Transition Planner}\label{sec:planner}

The operator hands the runtime \(p_1\), the presently running policy, and \(p_2\), the one they now want.
The transition planner's job is to propose a \((\varphi ; \delta)^{*}\) sequence whose \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level fold takes \(p_1\) to some \(p_2'\) with \(p_2' \eqR p_2\).
The operator accepts or rejects the proposal; they do not author it directly.

Every sequence the planner emits is safe by the per-form guarantees of \cref{sec:per-form}, aggregated across the sequence as sketched in \cref{sec:idioms}, regardless of how the planner assembled it.
The planner aims to land at \(p_2\) by construction: each case below emits a \(\delta\) or idiom whose \(\psem{\cdot}\) realizes the locally-observed \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level difference.
A misstep in the case analysis could in principle yield a sequence whose fold \(\psem{\delta_n} \circ \ldots \circ \psem{\delta_1} (p_1)\) misses \(p_2\)'s \(\eqR\)-class.
The \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level check of \cref{sec:idioms} catches this, and rejects any such sequence before it is installed.
Optimality is a separate matter, and is not guaranteed, since the planner may fail to see a better sequence that the operator has in mind.

\cref{sec:fallback} sets up \emph{confinement} as the metric we judge a plan by, presents the always-available worst-case \Replace{} fallback, and frames the planner's strategy as descending the tree to find a tight form (\Add, \Remove, or \ChangeMeta) that captures the difference locally.
\cref{sec:planner-emits} catalogs the cases the planner recognizes and emits a tight sequence for.
\cref{sec:imperative} shows how an operator can instead author a sequence by hand, admitted by the same \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level check.

\subsection{The Always-Available Fallback, and Confinement to do Better}\label{sec:fallback}

To reach any \(p_2\) from any \(p_1\), the planner can issue \(\Replace([], p_2)\), the \cref{sec:idioms} idiom that expands to

\[
\begin{array}{@{}l@{}}
(\mathtt{true};\ \Designate([], p_2))\ ;\ (\mathtt{true};\ \Quiesce([0]))\ ;\\[3pt]
\qquad (\mathtt{isEmpty}([0]);\ \Undesignate([]))
\end{array}
\]
This wraps \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) into \(\Strictstar(\mathtt{hi}: p_1, \mathtt{lo}: p_2)\) at the root (so pops still drain \(p_1\) while pushes go to \(p_2\)), and, once \(p_1\) is empty, collapses the wrapper to leave \(p_2\) alone.
Nothing is dropped and the sequence is safe by \cref{sec:per-form}, but \emph{no part of the scheduler is left undisturbed}.

\paragraph{Footprint}

To measure how much of the tree a sequence touches, associate each form \(\delta\) with a \emph{footprint} on the live control it acts on.
Define \(\mathtt{footprint}(\delta, C)\) as the set of nodes of \(C\) whose \ensuremath{\mathit{state}}, \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}, or \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} differs between \(C\) and \(\csem{\delta}(C)\).
The set is read off the \(\csem{\delta}\) rules of \cref{sec:edit-semantics}, each of which names every field of every node it produces.
It includes the edit site, the new or removed node where applicable, and every proper ancestor whose \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} is extended or restricted, such as the ancestors touched by \Add, \Quiesce, \Remove, and \Designate.
For a guarded sequence \((\varphi_0 ; \delta_0) ; \ldots ; (\varphi_n ; \delta_n)\), the sequence-level footprint is \(\bigcup_i \mathtt{footprint}(\delta_i, \mathit{link}_i)\).
The root-level \(\Replace([], p_2)\) above has the maximum possible footprint: the whole tree.

\paragraph{The planner's strategy}

The metric by which we judge the planner is \emph{confinement}.
A good plan disturbs as little of the running scheduler as possible, leaving the parts of the tree that did not need to change still running.
A sequence is well-confined when its footprint is small relative to the symmetric \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level difference between \(p_1\) and \(p_2\).

The planner's goal is to recognize whether the difference between \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) is captured by a tight form from \cref{sec:delta-grammar} (an \Add, \Remove, or \ChangeMeta) and to emit that form wherever in the tree it sits, leaving everything else untouched.
The planner descends through the tree as long as \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) agree at the current level, looking for a recognized shape one level down.
When it finds one, it emits the matching form at that depth.
Only when the descent ends without a match does the planner fall back to \Replace.
By then the recursion has localized the difference to the deepest slot at which the policies disagree, so the resulting \Replace{} wraps only that slot.

Suppose
\[
p_1 = P(a, Q(b, c))
\]
for some scheduling disciplines \(P\) and \(Q\), and the operator requests
\[
p_2 = P(a, Q(b, c, d)).
\]
The planner has two ways to realize this, shown in \cref{fig:planner-add-vs-replace}.

Option (a) wraps the entire running tree under a fresh \(\Strictstar\) and grows a freshly compiled \(p_2\) alongside.
Every node sits inside that \(\Strictstar\), and the original \(p_1\), with all its in-flight packets, drains on the high-priority arm.
The footprint is the whole tree.

Option (b) extends \(Q\)'s arity by one and attaches a freshly compiled \(d\) leaf as the third arm.
The \(P\) and \(Q\) ancestors have only their \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} extended, with no packet movement and no state reset, and the \(a\) leaf is left undisturbed.
The footprint is the \(Q\) node and its single ancestor.

Much of the tree is left alone under option (b).
The case analysis of \cref{sec:planner-emits} is built precisely to find option (b) when it exists, and falls back to a \Replace{} (at the deepest position the descent reached) only when no such tight form captures the difference.

We make no claim that the planner's choice is canonical or minimal.
We claim only that whatever sequence it emits is safe (\cref{sec:per-form}) and no worse than the root-level fallback.

\subsection{The Planner's Case Analysis}\label{sec:planner-emits}

The planner has two parts: a top-level pass over \((p_1, p_2)\) as whole trees, and a \emph{per-level comparator} that the pass defers to.
Both compare policies up to \(\eqR\) by comparing canonical representatives, and we call the pass that computes a representative \emph{normalization}.
The comparator matches the two roots and then pairs up their child lists, emitting an \Add{} for an unpaired arm of \(p_2\), a \Retire{} for an unpaired arm of \(p_1\), a \ChangeMeta{} for a pair whose metadata alone differs, and a recursion into any pair whose arms differ.
It pairs the arms by the tightest of three strategies that applies: by slot at equal arity, by in-order sub-sequence at unequal arity, and, when neither child list embeds in the other, by the flows the arms carry.
Only when no strategy pairs the lists does the comparator give up and emit a \Replace{} at the position the recursion has reached.
Every recursion descends through a slot at which the two trees already agree, so that \Replace{} lands at the deepest slot to which the planner could isolate the difference, and the root-level \(\Replace([], p_2)\) of \cref{sec:fallback} is reached only when the comparator gives up at the root.
\cref{app:planner-emits} gives the case analysis in full, with a worked example.

\subsection{Imperative Authoring}\label{sec:imperative}

The transition planner so far has realized what we call \emph{declarative} authoring.
The operator states a starting policy \(p_1\) and a target \(p_2\), and the planner proposes a guarded sequence that achieves the change, which the operator either accepts, in which case we apply it to the running control, or declines.
Operators may instead author \emph{imperatively}, writing their desired \(p_2\) together with a \((\varphi; \delta)^{*}\) sequence intended to reach it, possibly built from idioms (\cref{sec:idioms}).
Imperative authoring gives the operator hands-on control over the transitionary policies the live control passes through, rather than delegating their choice to the planner.
An operator who already knows the transition they want can hand it over directly, instead of naming a destination and leaving the route to the case analysis of \cref{sec:planner-emits}.
The sequence they write may be better confined than the one the planner would have found, since the planner may fail to see it, and it may equally be worse.
The check below admits either, since it certifies that a sequence arrives and says nothing about what it cost.

The two modes produce sequences over the same grammar, discharge the same per-form checks of \cref{sec:per-form}, and are admitted by the same \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level check of \cref{sec:idioms}.
The paths in a hand-written sequence resolve against the running control rather than against the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} the operator wrote, which is why we echo \(\lfloor C \rfloor\) back to them (\cref{sec:interplay}).
An operator who reads a path off their own \(p_2\) can name a different arm than they meant, and the runtime has no way to detect it.

Before committing an operator-authored sequence we run it through the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}-level check of \cref{sec:idioms}.
Starting from \(\lfloor C \rfloor\), the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} we echoed back, we fold each \(\psem{\delta_i}\) along the sequence, and we admit it when every \(\psem{\delta_i}\) is defined on the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} it meets and the fold lands at some \(p_2'\) with \(p_2' \eqR p_2\).
Idioms are expanded before the fold runs, so a sequence written with \Retire{} and \Replace{} is checked as the \(\delta\)s they stand for, and naming a run of edits buys the operator brevity rather than a weaker obligation.
A rejection names the step that caused it, either the first \(\delta_i\) whose \(\psem{\delta_i}\) was undefined or else the final mismatch against \(p_2\).
An operator debugging a sequence of a dozen steps needs to know which one failed, and the fold supplies it without extra work.

Each intermediate \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} that the fold produces has an explainable scheduling semantics.
After the first step of \Replace(\ensuremath{\mathit{path}}, \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}})'s expansion, for instance, the intermediate \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} holds \(\Strict(\mathit{pol}@\mathit{path},\ \mathit{survivor})\) at \ensuremath{\mathit{path}}.
That is a temporary strict-priority node favoring the outgoing \(\mathit{pol}@\mathit{path}\) over the incoming \ensuremath{\mathit{survivor}}, and it has a readable scheduling interpretation in its own right.

\section{Rio Hardware Implementation}\label{sec:hardware}

\cref{sec:hw-substrate} describes the substrate a PIFO tree runs on today, and states the one property Rio needs that such a substrate does not offer: a commit that installs a whole list of instructions atomically with respect to traffic.
\cref{sec:hw-commit} is the change that supplies it, and \cref{sec:hw-survivor} adds the one piece of substrate machinery with no counterpart in the model, the designated-survivor link.
\cref{sec:hw-isa} gives the instruction set a commit is drawn from, \cref{sec:hw-compile} lowers each form of \(\delta\) to one commit, and \cref{sec:substrate-portability} separates what Rio needs from what our own substrate happens to do.

\subsection{The Substrate as it Stands}\label{sec:hw-substrate}

A PIFO tree is a scheduling abstraction, and a scheduler must be laid out on top of existing silicon.
Existing designs~\cite{Sivaraman16,Yao23,vPIFO} therefore build the tree out of programmable processing elements, or PEs, each hosting several PIFOs, one per node of the tree it carries.
Each PE carries hardware, usually match-action pipelines, that assigns a packet to one of its PIFOs and computes the packet's rank and next-hop destination.
Behind that, a single physical PIFO supplies the storage that all of the PE's PIFOs share.

We call the hardware-level realizations of \push{} and \pop{} \emph{enqueue} and \emph{dequeue}, and on this substrate both are pipelined and non-atomic.
On enqueue, the switch must compute two pieces of information before issuing PE pushes: which PIFO the packet should enter, and what rank should be inserted at each of those PIFOs.
Classification determines the first, by mapping a flow to a child.
The PIFO's scheduling policy computes the second, from packet fields, flow state, and per-arm metadata.
For example, a \WFQ{} PIFO uses the child weight to compute a virtual finish time, while a \Strict{} PIFO interprets the metadata as a priority rank.
Both are computed by match-action tables~\cite{Bosshart14}, so installing a policy on such a substrate means writing entries into those tables.
On dequeue, the switch starts from the output port's root PIFO, pops the best-ranked entry, and follows the stored next-hop information until it reaches a packet to transmit or another PIFO to pop.

This is sufficient for a static policy, but it does not by itself support dynamic policy updates concurrent with packet enqueue and dequeue.
In such a case, a single packet operation can observe a mixture of old and new scheduler state.
For example, an enqueue may choose its path using the old flow-to-child mapping but compute its rank using newly updated per-arm metadata, or a dequeue may pop from one version of the tree and then follow next-hop state from another.
As a result, existing PIFO-based substrates support only stop-the-world policy update~\cite{vPIFO,Alcoz23}.

Rio asks a substrate for one thing: commits that are atomic with respect to traffic.
A \(\delta\) that lowers to many instructions has to install as a single instant.
The tree is transiently malformed partway through a commit, and no \push{} or \pop{} may observe it in that state.
That is the whole of what \cref{sec:hw-preservation}'s argument rests on.

Our substrate is deliberately unambitious; we built it because the compiler needed something to run against and we wanted to understand precisely what features we needed from our substrate.

\subsection{Making a Commit Atomic}\label{sec:hw-commit}

Rio implements a transactional update controller, and every commit passes through it.
A commit reaches the controller as a stream of update instructions terminated by a commit marker, and the controller executes that stream in order.
Ordinary update instructions are routed to the PEs they target and staged locally, where they remain invisible to packet processing.
When the controller reaches the marker, it generates the hardware commit signals that make the staged updates visible.
Two pieces of hardware need small changes for those signals to be atomic: the tables that hold a node's classification, and the rank computation.

Rio builds transactional tables for a node's classification.
Each PE holds the set of flows each of its nodes admits, and the mapping from an admitted flow to the child that serves it, in a specialized structure that accepts a stream of update instructions followed by a commit marker.
Only when the marker is applied does the new version become active.
We implement this using double buffering.
An update instruction modifies a shadow copy of the table, which packet processing does not read, and the commit signal atomically swaps the active and shadow copies.
After the swap, the inactive copy is synchronized in the background to serve as the shadow for the next commit.
Staging updates in a shadow copy and swapping them in is also how runtime reconfiguration is done for the forwarding pipeline~\cite{Xing22,Yang24}.
Rio therefore asks its substrate for no primitive that runtime-programmable switch designs do not already provide.

A swap is atomic within one PE, but a commit generally spans several, and a packet in flight visits them in sequence.
The commit signals are therefore delay-aligned across PEs according to the PIFO tree topology, so that no enqueue or dequeue operation can traverse a partially updated tree.
The controller treats the commit boundary as a wavefront.
The new configuration becomes visible at different PEs at carefully chosen times, so that any individual packet observes a consistent configuration along its entire path.
The double buffer and the wavefront are two mechanisms.
Their shared purpose is pipelined dequeue, so that a request travelling the chain of PEs never observes state that a commit has left inconsistent.

Rio supports incremental updates to the rank-computing match-action tables, and does not require arbitrary in-place replacement of a live scheduling policy.
Such an update either installs metadata for a flow whose packets are not admitted until the commit finishes, or retires metadata for a flow that has already drained, so it never changes the interpretation of a packet already in flight.
Our controller stages and swaps these writes along with every other instruction in their commit, so the metadata an \Add{} carries lands in the same atomic instant as the structural change it accompanies.
Rio does not require that, since the table whose commit must be a single instant is the one mapping a flow to the PIFO that serves it, and that mapping is what admits a packet at all.
The rank computation behind it is a reference design, and a substrate may configure its own disciplines by whatever means it has.

The controller also implements guarded transitions.
Some transitions are guarded by predicates such as the emptiness of a subtree (\cref{sec:sequences}).
Rio lowers such guards to blocking commands in the controller queue.
A blocking command remains at the head of the queue until its predicate becomes true.
Only then can the subsequent update instructions execute.
In-order execution therefore ensures that instructions depending on the guard are not issued prematurely.

Our current implementation uses a single controller queue, which provides a simple global serialization point for updates.
This design is sufficient for correctness and avoids subtle ordering bugs between overlapping commits.
It can, however, introduce head-of-line blocking when a guarded transition waits for a long-running drain.
Supporting multiple independent queues for disjoint subtrees is a natural optimization, and it changes neither Rio's ISA nor its correctness argument.
We leave that extension to future work.

\subsection{Designated Survivors}\label{sec:hw-survivor}

A \(\Strictstar\) node is realized not as a PIFO but as a \emph{designated-survivor link}, a pointer from the node it replaces to the subtree replacing it.
This subsection says why the link is worth having and how a \push{} and a \pop{} each reach the survivor through it.

\cref{sec:policy-dsl} offers \(\Strictstar\) as a discipline of its own even though it schedules exactly as the 2-ary \Strict{} does.
The difference is that a \(\Strictstar\) node needs no \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}, and a node that needs no \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} needs no \emph{level}.
A PE holds one level of the tree, so every node that must be stored somewhere costs a level for itself and everything beneath it.

A substrate that offers \(\Strictstar\) guarantees two things of it.
Arm 0 is drained before arm 1 is served, and the two arms' priorities never change.
An ordinary \Strict{} can promise only the first.
A \ChangeMeta{} may invert its priorities under a standing backlog, and ranks are frozen at \push{} time (\cref{sec:nondisruption}) so that the backlog survives the inversion, which is what the node's \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} is for.
Nothing can invert a \(\Strictstar\): \ChangeMeta{} may not target its arms (\cref{sec:changemeta}), and nothing else disturbs it between the \Designate{} that creates it and the \Undesignate{} that retires it.
The drain order therefore holds at every instant of the node's life, and a \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} there would record nothing the two arms do not already record.

\paragraph{What dropping the \texorpdfstring{\ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}}}{pifo} saves}

Suppose the outgoing subtree has height two on levels 5 and 6, and a survivor of height three is to replace it from level 5 beside it.
A literal \Strict-2 wrapper holds a \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} and so has to live somewhere.
It takes level 5, pushing the outgoing subtree to 6 and 7 and the survivor to 6 through 8, and retiring it afterwards pulls the survivor back up.
Two subtrees are laid out twice over, for a node that exists only for the length of a drain.

\paragraph{The designated-survivor link}

Every PIFO carries a \emph{designated-survivor link}, which is left null in an ordinary node.
A \Designate{} points the outgoing node \texttt{v}'s link at the survivor's root \texttt{surv}.
The survivor is laid out beside \texttt{v} rather than beneath it, and \texttt{v}'s parent is not told.
It keeps the one index it had.
A designated survivor occupies its designator's physical position, so the two roots share a PE, and a model path reaches a physical level by discarding the \(\Strictstar\) nodes above it.

\emph{A \push{} reaches the survivor directly.}
The admitting chain for one of the survivor's flows runs from the root, through the parent, into the survivor, and down to a leaf.
\texttt{v} is not on that chain, because \Designate{} stopped \texttt{v} admitting those flows.
Every node on the chain mints one index, exactly as it would for an \Add, and the link is never consulted.
No node classifies anything it was not classifying already, which is why the construction needs no table of its own.

\emph{A \pop{} reaches the survivor only through \texttt{v}.}
The parent spends an index on that slot and queries \texttt{v}.
While \texttt{v} holds packets, it returns packets.
Once \texttt{v} is empty it defers to its designated survivor, and the query passes to \texttt{surv} rather than underflowing.

The parent therefore holds one index per packet in \emph{either} subtree, and spends them on \texttt{v} until \texttt{v} is dry and on \texttt{surv} thereafter, which is the arm-0-before-arm-1 order \cref{sec:wrap-nondisruption} proves non-disruptive.
The elision does change what \cref{sec:controls}'s well-formedness ranges over.
That invariant counts, at each node, one \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} entry per packet in the subtree under the corresponding child, and here the parent holds entries for packets in a subtree that is not its child at all.
This count is still correct once ``the subtree under index \(i\)'' is read as the subtree at \(i\) together with its designated survivor's, and an index that leads to \texttt{v} when \texttt{v} is empty does not actually reach a leaf with nothing to emit, since it reaches \texttt{surv}.
At the \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} level no reading is needed, since \(\mathtt{wrap}\) (\cref{sec:designate}) builds a real \(\Strictstar\) node with two real children and a \ensuremath{\mathit{pifo}} of its own, and \cref{sec:controls} applies to it word for word.

\paragraph{Ending the transition}

The indirection has a cost.
Once \texttt{v} has drained, every \pop{} that reaches that slot queries \texttt{v}, gets nothing, and queries again, at the tree level where the two roots sit.
So the transition has to end rather than merely settle.
\cref{sec:sequences} pairs \Undesignate{} with the guard \(\mathtt{isEmpty}(\mathit{path})\), and \cref{sec:hw-commit} lowers such a guard to a blocking command in the controller queue.
The commit sits at the head of that queue until the outgoing subtree drains, and then fires atomically.
After it the parent's index reaches the survivor directly, and nothing in the tree records that a transition happened.

An operator may also put a \(\Strictstar\) in a policy of their own (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}), and \(\lceil \cdot \rceil\) compiles it to what \Designate{} builds, laying out both arms and pointing the first arm's link at the second.

\subsection{The Commit Interface and its ISA}\label{sec:hw-isa}

Rio enables online policy update by packaging many hardware state updates into a single \emph{commit}, and the ISA the substrate exposes has exactly one unit.
A \texttt{commit} is a list of instructions that the substrate installs \emph{atomically}.
Every enqueue or dequeue observes either the whole list applied, or none of it.
The entire list lands between two consecutive \push{}/\pop{} operations, with no intermediate state visible to the network's \pop{} stream.
This is the atomicity that \cref{sec:delta-grammar} demanded of every form it admits.
A commit's \emph{length} may depend on live control state (how many flows are admitted, how many siblings need shifting), but its \emph{atomicity} does not.

Each \(\delta\) (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}) lowers to one \texttt{commit}.
The ISA itself is unconditional.
The substrate trusts that its caller has issued a well-formed commit.
Any situation-dependent reasoning, such as ``the subtree we are about to collapse is empty'', lives at \cref{sec:sequences}, in a guard \(\varphi\) that had to become true before the corresponding \(\delta\) fired.

The ISA has thirteen opcodes, laid out in \cref{tab:isa-opcodes}.
They address a PIFO by an opaque id \texttt{v}, one per tree node, and one of its arms by an \emph{index}, an opaque per-PIFO handle for a child.

\texttt{change\_arity} carries the one subtlety in the table.
It tells the discipline-level logic at \texttt{v} how many arms are active, and growing the count creates the new slot, which the substrate seeds from \texttt{v}'s current node state by \(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}\) (\cref{sec:compilation}).
The structural attaching and detaching happen separately, through \texttt{adopt} and \texttt{emancipate}, each keyed by the stable per-edge index the parent minted at adoption time.

Our single-purpose opcodes also mean that the tree can become transiently malformed \emph{within} a commit, in ways that the commit's atomicity hides from any observer.
For an example from \cref{sec:hw-compile}, \Add{}'s commit raises the target's arity to \texttt{n+1} before the \texttt{adopt} that actually attaches a child.
The atomic install means no \texttt{push} or \texttt{pop} ever sees this state, but the planner is free to issue commits whose intermediate states would not satisfy the well-formedness conditions of \cref{sec:controls}.

The ISA is the compiler's interface, and not all of the state it names reaches the switch.
Per-edge indices and arities are how the compiler addresses a tree position, and a PE stores neither.
A PE stores its \texttt{Assoc} set, a \texttt{Map} naming the PIFO each admitted flow enters and the PE that holds it, and the designated-survivor link of \cref{sec:hw-survivor}.
An index is meaningful only against the tree the compiler holds, so an \texttt{adopt} reaches the switch only by changing which PIFO an index names.

Which PE holds a given PIFO is the substrate's choice, written \texttt{pe(path)} and fixed at compile time as a function of the PIFO's position in the tree.
A typical choice is depth-by-PE, where all PIFOs at one depth share a PE~\cite{Sivaraman16}, so siblings do too.
The planner consults \texttt{pe(path)} for a \texttt{spawn}'s \texttt{pe} argument and cannot otherwise observe it.

\subsection{Compiling Policy Edits}\label{sec:hw-compile}

Each form of \(\delta\) (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}) lowers to a list of opcodes.
The lowering \emph{schema} is mechanical.
Each form names a fixed sequence of opcodes, and the planner fills in the schema's positional parameters, such as flow lists, chain shapes, and routing indices.

The planner resolves those parameters when it plans the sequence, rather than when a commit reaches the substrate.
It reads them off its own model of the tree, which is host-side state that no PE holds.
Nothing that happens while the sequence runs can invalidate them.
A lowering reads tree shape, flow sets and arities.
Only traffic runs while a guard waits, and traffic changes none of those.
The whole of a guarded sequence can therefore be dispatched at once, and \cref{sec:hw-commit}'s controller queue holds it as fully resolved commits.

A switch with \(P\) output ports has \(P\) separate trees, each wrapped at the ISA in a reserved 1-arm PIFO \texttt{port\_root} whose sole child, reached through the index \texttt{port\_step}, is the actual tree root.
Its \texttt{Assoc} and \texttt{Map} mirror the actual root's, since every walk below begins there.
The port root is what makes the tree root an ordinary position.
\texttt{undesignate} retargets a parent's index from a PIFO to that PIFO's designated survivor, and at the root there is no parent's index to retarget.
That case is not hypothetical: \cref{sec:fallback}'s always-available plan is \(\Replace([], p_2)\), whose last step is \(\Undesignate([])\).
Without the port root the substrate could not execute the one transition the planner is guaranteed to be able to fall back on, and would need a second opcode meaning ``make this PIFO the tree root'', doing at one position what \texttt{undesignate} does everywhere else.
\Designate{} itself needs no parent, since it only writes the outgoing node's own link.
It is \Undesignate{} rather than \Designate{} that wants a slot above.

The three parts of \ensuremath{\mathit{z}} (\cref{sec:controls}) line up one to one with this vocabulary.
A node's \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{admit}\) is its \texttt{Assoc} set, its \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{serves}\) is its \texttt{Map} table, its \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{rank}\) is what \texttt{set\_policy} installs and \texttt{discipline(v)} reads back, and its \(\mathit{z}.\mathit{meta}\) is what \texttt{set\_arm\_meta} writes.
Each of \ensuremath{\mathit{z}}'s parts is thus an opcode's target, and none of them is state that \texttt{push} or \texttt{pop} advances.

A tree position and the PIFO sitting at it are different things, so each lowering binds a name to the PIFO that the form targets, and speaks of that PIFO thereafter.
Queries against a PIFO \texttt{v}, its \texttt{arity(v)} and \texttt{discipline(v)}, are read off the planner's model of the tree as it stands at that point in the sequence, and a parameter an opcode leaves unmentioned is \texttt{v}'s value there.
\texttt{spawn}'s elided \texttt{pe} argument is \texttt{pe(pos(v))}.

Two notations recur.
\texttt{foreach x in S} emits its body once per \texttt{x}, and \texttt{walk(op, C, f)} emits \texttt{op(v, f, \ldots)} at each \(v \in C\), reading positional remainders such as \texttt{map}'s routing index off that same model.
Of the queries, \texttt{flows(v)} is the set of flows admitted beneath \texttt{v}, and \texttt{chain(f)} runs from \texttt{port\_root} to the leaf admitting \texttt{f}, with \texttt{internals(f)} that chain minus the leaf.
For a compiled subtree \texttt{T}, \texttt{nodes(T)} is its PIFOs and \texttt{root(T)} its root, and \texttt{layout(T)} spawns and configures all of \texttt{T}, leaving the parent-side \texttt{adopt} of \(\mathtt{root(T)}\) to the caller.

We write \ensuremath{\mathit{path}} for the form's path argument, \texttt{parent} for the PIFO at the position above it, and \texttt{k} for its index at that PIFO.
For \(\mathit{path} = [\,]\), \texttt{parent} is \texttt{port\_root} and \texttt{k} is \texttt{port\_step}.
Three of the forms are written below with their argument split as \(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k]\) rather than as \ensuremath{\mathit{path}}, because their lowerings address the parent's slot rather than the node itself, and so need \texttt{k} by name.

\paragraph{\(\Add(\mathit{path}, D, \mathit{arm}, m)\)}
\begin{Verbatim}
v := PIFO at path
n := arity(v)
layout(arm)
change_arity(v, n+1)
set_arm_meta(v, n, m)
adopt(n, v, root(arm))
foreach f in flows(arm):
    walk(assoc, chain(f), f)
    walk(map,   internals(f), f)
\end{Verbatim}
The new subtree occupies \texttt{v}'s rightmost slot (index \texttt{n}), so existing children's indices are undisturbed.

\paragraph{\(\ChangeMeta(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k], D, m)\)}
\begin{Verbatim}
set_arm_meta(parent, k, m)
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{\(\Quiesce(\mathit{path})\)}
\begin{Verbatim}
v := PIFO at path
foreach f in flows(v):
    walk(deassoc, chain(f), f)
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{\(\Remove(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k])\)}
\Remove{} is \Add{} run backwards.
A \texttt{change\_arity} that decrements \texttt{parent}'s count and an \texttt{emancipate(k, parent)} detach the arm, an \texttt{unmap} walk over \(\mathtt{internals(f)}\) for each \(f \in \mathtt{flows(v)}\) forgets its routing, and a \texttt{gc} at every PIFO of the departing subtree releases the PE slots.
The \(\sigma_k\) renumbering of \cref{sec:remove} has no counterpart here, since a child is reached by an opaque per-edge index that \texttt{parent} minted when it adopted that child, so dropping one child leaves every sibling's index as it was.

\paragraph{\(\Designate(\mathit{path}, \mathit{survivor})\)}
\begin{Verbatim}
loser := PIFO at path
surv  := root(survivor)
layout(survivor)
designate(loser, surv)
foreach f in flows(loser) & flows(surv):
    walk(deassoc, chain(f) & nodes(loser), f)
foreach f in flows(surv):
    walk(assoc, chain(f), f)
    walk(map,   internals(f), f)
\end{Verbatim}
This is \Add{}'s lowering, with \texttt{designate} in place of \texttt{change\_arity} and \texttt{adopt} and one walk in front.
No PIFO is spawned for the \(\Strictstar\) node, and \texttt{loser}'s parent is not touched.
\texttt{designate} points \texttt{loser}'s designated-survivor link at \texttt{surv} (\cref{sec:hw-survivor}).
The two walks are the whole of the traffic handover.

The deassoc walk hands the shared flows over to the survivor.
Both subtrees can serve a shared flow, and the two roots occupy one position, so the losing arm must stop admitting it.
The deassociation runs before the assoc walk because \(\mathtt{chain(f)}\) resolves through \texttt{loser} only while \texttt{loser} still admits \texttt{f}.

The assoc and map walks are the same as for \Add{}.
For a shared flow the chain above the two roots is already in place and the walks rewrite it to no effect.
Flows in \(\mathtt{flows(loser)} \setminus \mathtt{flows(surv)}\) are left alone entirely, and go on reaching \texttt{loser}'s own subtree, matching \cref{sec:designate}'s rule that loser-only labels drain on arm 0.
Silencing those, when the operator ultimately wants it, comes from the \Quiesce{} step of the surrounding \Replace{} idiom (\cref{sec:sequences}), not from \Designate.

\paragraph{\(\Undesignate(\pi \mathbin{+\!\!+} [k])\)}
\Undesignate{} is likewise \Designate{} run backwards.
A single \texttt{undesignate(loser)} retargets \texttt{parent}'s index \texttt{k} from \texttt{loser} to \texttt{surv}, the flows in \(\mathtt{flows(loser)} \setminus \mathtt{flows(surv)}\) are unmapped along their internals, and \texttt{loser}'s PIFOs are released.
Reusing \texttt{k} is what preserves \texttt{parent}'s per-arm metadata for that slot, so the survivor inherits the annotation that the outgoing subtree held.
The shared flows are deliberately not unmapped, since they serve \texttt{surv} now.

\Quiesce{}'s wide reach, covering the root-to-\ensuremath{\mathit{path}} chain along with the whole subtree under \texttt{v}, is forced by \cref{sec:policy-dsl}'s parallel push.
Every node on a packet's path mints its own routing index independently, so dropping \texttt{f} at only some of those nodes would leave the rest minting stray indices, and the tree malformed.
\Quiesce{} does not issue \texttt{unmap}.
A silenced flow's Map entries \emph{must outlive the silencing} so that buffered packets can be popped.
\Remove{} pairs \texttt{emancipate} with \texttt{unmap} precisely because it fires after the drain, when there is no longer any in-flight traffic to preserve.
A \Quiesce{} that fires in the middle of a \Replace, on the outgoing arm of a \(\Strictstar\), restricts its deassoc walk to loser-only flows, since \Designate{} routed the shared flows to the survivor at commit time (\cref{sec:designate}), and de-associating them here would silence live traffic on the survivor.

\subsection{Hosting Rio on Another Substrate}\label{sec:substrate-portability}

The substrate also supplies the disciplines themselves.
\(\mathit{init\_node\_state}_{D}\), \(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}\) and \(\mathit{rank}_{D}\) are the substrate's (\cref{sec:compilation}), and the menu it implements is the menu an operator may write from.
That is a parameter, since a substrate with a shorter menu hosts Rio just as well over a smaller \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}}.
If the network operator wishes to install a discipline that the menu lacks, they need to supply the substrate with an \(\mathit{init\_node\_state}_{D}\), an \(\mathit{init\_slot\_state}_{D}\) and a \(\mathit{rank}_{D}\) of their own.
A small programmable core beside the PIFO tree would be one way to supply this.

The designated-survivor link of \cref{sec:hw-survivor} is an optimization rather than a requirement, since a substrate without one lowers \Designate{} to a literal \Strict-2 wrapper and pays for the relayouts.
The bar for hosting Rio is therefore a single property, and we expect other PIFO substrates to clear it and to be able to adopt the link besides~\cite{Shrivastav19,Gao22,Yao23,vPIFO,Atre24,Alcoz25}.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Consistent network update}

The work closest to ours in spirit concerns packet \emph{forwarding} rather than packet scheduling.
An operator who changes the forwarding rules across a set of devices cannot install them everywhere at once, so packets are in flight through the network while the change is spreading.
\citet{Reitblatt12} observe that such an update passes through intermediate configurations, and that those configurations can behave in ways that neither the old configuration nor the new one would.
Our work makes that same observation about scheduling.
Their answer is a versioning discipline, which yields two consistency levels, per-packet and per-flow: a packet is stamped on entry and handled under the stamped version at every device, so one configuration handles it from end to end.
Rio reaches per-packet consistency by another means: the entire commit lands between two consecutive \push{}/\pop{} operations, so a packet meets one configuration or the other (\cref{sec:hw-isa}).

The two settings differ in what the old configuration holds.
In forwarding, a forwarding rule holds no traffic, so the old configuration can be retired once the packets stamped with it have left the network.
In scheduling, a scheduler node holds packets whose ranks were fixed when they were \push{}ed, and no version tag can re-sort them.
Rio therefore retires the old configuration by draining it, which is what a guard waits for (\cref{sec:idioms}).

\citet{Mahajan13} study the dependencies that a consistency property imposes among rule updates, and find that stronger properties induce more intricate dependencies and therefore slower updates.
\citet{Namjoshi24} keep the guarantee and drop the versioning, sequencing the updates so that route consistency holds with a single table edited in place.
Our guarded sequences are dependency structures of the same kind, drawn inside one scheduler.
While their dependencies run between rules on separate devices, ours run between edits at different positions of one tree.

All of that work rewrites the entries that a device's tables hold.
FlexCore~\cite{Xing22} reconfigures the \emph{program} those tables belong to: it changes a live data plane's match-action tables, control flow, and parser.
It computes a reconfiguration script, which is a sequence of atomic graph edits that carries the old program to the new one, and defines three consistency guarantees over the intermediate states along the way.
Our guarded sequences (\cref{sec:sequences}) have the same shape.
This similarity is unsurprising: changing the structure that a packet passes through, without first stopping the traffic, calls for atomic steps and an account of what holds between them, in either their domain (forwarding) or ours (scheduling).
The strongest of their guarantees, program consistency, lets no intermediate state reach a packet, and a Rio commit provides that for a single \(\delta\) (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}).
The two domains diverge over what is being changed.
A match-action table holds no packet, and FlexCore does not handle stateful programs, leaving for future work how state should be ported to a new program.
A packet scheduler is exactly such a stateful case, since its entire content is buffered packets whose ranks were fixed at \push{} time.
Reconfiguring a packet scheduler is therefore what FlexCore sets aside, and what this paper takes up.

These updates run through the interface that P4 calls \emph{population}, the insertion and removal of match-action table entries~\cite{Bosshart14}.
Population determines the policy applied to packets at any given instant.
P4 anticipates reconfiguration that does not interrupt forwarding, and leaves the mechanism to implementations.
\citet{Yang24} take that interface as far as it goes, compiling whole runtime programs down to table entries and installing them without disrupting traffic.
Rio's commit is a group of such entry writes that installs together, so that the tables that a packet consults along its path never disagree about which policy is in force (\cref{sec:hw-commit}).

\paragraph{Reconfiguring a live scheduler}

Loom schedules at the NIC over a policy written as a restricted DAG, and it reconfigures that hierarchy while traffic flows~\cite{Stephens19}.
Configuration values travel inline with packet descriptors and doorbells, and each stage of the match-action pipeline saves the values it is given to its local memory as it processes them.
A descriptor and the configuration it carries therefore reach each stage together, which is the effect Rio obtains by delay-aligning commit signals across PEs (\cref{sec:hw-commit}).
Loom does not say what becomes of the packets already buffered under a node whose configuration changes, and the interval in which an update spreads through the pipeline is given no scheduling meaning.

SP-PIFO also changes its scheduler as it runs, adapting the mapping from packet ranks to a fixed set of strict-priority queues as traffic shifts~\cite{Alcoz20}.
That adaptation is measured as error against an ideal PIFO, so what SP-PIFO converges on is the policy the operator already wrote.
Rio instead changes that policy, and reports the change back in the operator's own DSL (\cref{sec:equivalences}).

\citet{Alcoz23} raise the question directly.
Their scheduling hypervisor deploys per-tenant scheduling policies onto one switch, and they ask how to move from one such policy to another as tenants arrive and depart.
They name two obstacles, emptying the buffers and resetting the state of the scheduling functions, and leave the mechanism to future runtime-programmable data planes.
Rio answers the first by draining rather than dropping (\cref{sec:quiesce}), and the second by declining to reset (\cref{sec:changemeta}).

Reconfiguration is routine in the schedulers deployed at hosts and NICs.
Loom updates rate limiters whenever a VM or a flow starts or stops~\cite{Stephens19}.
PicNIC reacts to a breakdown of isolation between tenants at sub-millisecond timescales~\cite{Kumar19}.
FairNIC gives each tenant an independent subtree of packet schedulers to configure~\cite{Grant20}, so the set of tenants fixes the shape of the tree.
Carousel shapes tens of thousands of flows and policies on a single server~\cite{Saeed17}.
Eiffel argues for scheduling in software on the grounds of a shorter development cycle~\cite{Saeed19}.

\paragraph{Programmable scheduling substrates}

\citet{Sivaraman16} introduce the PIFO and the PIFO tree, observing that a switch offers a small menu of scheduling algorithms, and that once the silicon is designed an operator can tune a parameter and nothing more.
PIEO generalizes the primitive to extraction from an arbitrary position in the queue~\cite{Shrivastav19}.
SP-PIFO approximates a PIFO with a stack of strict-priority queues on devices that ship today~\cite{Alcoz20}.
PACKS approximates a PIFO's scheduling order together with its admission control, where earlier approximations captured one at a time~\cite{Alcoz25}.
Exp-PIFO approximates one with adaptive exponential bins, keeping two memory cells of state~\cite{Mostafaei25}.
BMW Tree implements one as a balanced multi-way sorting tree that scales to large flow counts~\cite{Yao23}.
BBQ reaches hundreds of thousands of concurrent flows by carrying hierarchical find-first-set into a pipelined integer priority queue~\cite{Atre24}.
Gearbox approximates weighted fair queuing with a hierarchy of queuing levels at constant cost per operation~\cite{Gao22}.
vPIFO virtualizes a single physical PIFO into many instances that its PIFO Visor can wire into a tree of any shape~\cite{vPIFO}; we take the gap it leaves at runtime as a case study in \cref{sec:vpifo}.

All of these substrates fix the tree's shape and the discipline at each node when the scheduler is configured.
Rio runs above any of them and asks for one property: commits that are atomic with respect to traffic (\cref{sec:hw-substrate}).
Each is therefore a plausible host, and what it would have to add is a transactional path to the tables that our opcodes write.

One recent substrate offers more than Rio assumes.
UIFO adds a level of classes above packets, so that the service order of already-buffered traffic can be updated after the fact~\cite{Wang26}.
Rio's forms are defined against a substrate where a rank is fixed at \push{} time (\cref{sec:controls}), and our non-disruption theorem (\cref{thm:nondisruption}) is stated against that.
A substrate that can re-rank a backlog admits reconfigurations for which this grammar has no form.

\paragraph{Formal models of scheduling}

\citet{Mohan23} give a syntax and an operational semantics for PIFO trees, separating a tree's topology from the control that runs on it.
Our runtime model (\cref{sec:controls}) is adapted from theirs.
Their question is whether a policy written for one topology can be realized on a tree of a different shape, which they answer by embedding one topology in another.
Ours is how a tree may be changed while it runs, answered by our edits (\cref{sec:delta-grammar}), by their agreement with the policy the operator reads back (\cref{thm:interplay}), and by what they cost the backlog (\cref{thm:nondisruption}).

Embedding suggests a route to reconfiguration of its own: embed both \(p_1\)'s tree and \(p_2\)'s tree into a common target, and move between the two embeddings.
The backlog would be laid out under the first embedding and read under the second.
In our work, we decline to make such a scheduling decision on the operator's behalf (\cref{sec:remove}).

\citet{Campbell26} ask that same portability question of match-action tables rather than of trees.
They give table transformations an algebra and a type system under which every well-typed transformation denotes one realizable function, and compile a specification into concrete tables for a chosen target.
The object there is a table and the target is another environment, where ours is a tree and the target is the same tree a moment later.

\citet{Mittal16} ask whether a single scheduling algorithm can replay the schedule that any other algorithm produced, and find that in general none can.
That compares two schedulers by what they emit, which is how we compare a scheduler with itself across an edit (\cref{thm:nondisruption}).

\section{The Planner's Case Analysis in Full}\label{app:planner-emits}

This appendix expands \cref{sec:planner-emits}.

The planner has two parts: a top-level pass over \((p_1, p_2)\) as whole trees, and a \emph{per-level comparator} that the pass defers to.
Whatever the comparator emits is lifted back to the right depth by prepending the slot indices the recursion descended through.

Both parts compare policies up to \(\eqR\), by comparing canonical representatives.
Each \(\eqR\)-class has one under any fixed ordering of the arms at a node (\cref{sec:equivalences}), and we call the pass that computes it \emph{normalization}.
The planner normalizes both sides before it compares them, the operator's request and the policy it reads off the running control.
The read-off is not canonical on its own, since an edit may splice a new arm into whatever slot the control has free rather than into the slot normalization would have chosen.
So the planner keeps the permutation that normalizing the read-off applied, and the slot indices it prepends name slots of the running control rather than of the normalized term.

\paragraph{The top-level pass}

The pass tries two cases in order and stops at the first match.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \(p_1 = p_2\). Emit \([\,]\).
The test is literal equality on the normalized \ensuremath{\mathit{pol}} terms, so two \(\eqR\)-equivalent policies arrive here as identical terms.
The live control is already serving the requested policy and there is nothing to install.

\item Otherwise. Hand \((p_1, p_2)\) to the per-level comparator.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the comparator emits}

The comparator compares the roots of \(p_1\) and \(p_2\).
If the two roots run different disciplines, or are leaves carrying different flows, it emits a \Replace{}, the always-available idiom of \cref{sec:fallback}.
If the roots agree, the task reduces to \emph{pairing up the two child lists}, and each pairing yields one of four kinds of edit.
An arm of \(p_2\) with no partner in \(p_1\) is a fresh \Add{}; an arm of \(p_1\) with no partner in \(p_2\) is a \Retire{}.
A matched pair whose two arms are identical is set aside and contributes nothing.
A matched pair whose arms agree but whose slot metadata differs contributes a lone \ChangeMeta{}.
A matched pair whose arms differ starts a recursion into the pair to localize the change further, with a \ChangeMeta{} beside it when the metadata differs too.

Where that \ChangeMeta{} sits depends on what the recursion does to the slot.
Ranks freeze at \push{} time (\cref{sec:nondisruption}), so a \ChangeMeta{} governs only the packets that arrive after it fires.
When the recursion replaces the whole slot the \ChangeMeta{} goes first, since \Designate{} hands the slot to the survivor at once and the \Quiesce{} silences the old arm, so everything the slot accepts from that instant is traffic the new metadatum was written for, while the old subtree drains on ranks no \ChangeMeta{} rewrites.
When the recursion works strictly below the slot the \ChangeMeta{} goes last, since until the drain completes the slot goes on serving arms that are neither arriving nor departing, and re-tuning them early would hold them at the new metadatum for as long as the drain runs.

\paragraph{Pairing the child lists}

The comparator pairs the arms by the tightest strategy that applies, dropping to a more general one only when the tighter fails.
All three strategies lean on normalization.
Arms that genuinely correspond appear in the same relative order on both sides, which is what lets the positional strategies pair by position at all.

\textbf{By slot, at equal arity.}
When the child lists have the same length, the comparator pairs arm \(i\) with arm \(i\).
A slot whose arm and metadata both agree contributes nothing, and a slot whose metadata alone differs contributes a \ChangeMeta{}.
A slot whose arm differs recurses: it emits whatever the recursion into that arm localizes, and, when the slot's metadata also changed, a \ChangeMeta{} placed as above.
One shape is caught before the slot walk.
At \Strict{} or \WFQ{}, if \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) hold the same arms in a different order, the difference is a pure metadata reshuffle, such as a \Strict{} priority swap that normalization's rank ordering presents as a reordering.
The comparator then emits one \ChangeMeta{} per slot whose metadata changed.

\textbf{By sub-sequence, at unequal arity.}
When one child list embeds in the other as an in-order sub-sequence, the surplus arms are the whole of the change.
Normalization has already aligned the shared arms in a common order, so their appearing in order is the signal that none of them moved or changed and only the extra arms are new or gone.
If \(p_1\)'s arms are an in-order sub-sequence of \(p_2\)'s, each surplus arm of \(p_2\) is a fresh \Add{}.
\cref{sec:add}'s leaf-disjointness precondition holds automatically, since a surplus arm's leaves are leaves of \(p_2\) absent from \(p_1\) and hence disjoint by \cref{sec:policy-dsl}'s leaf-partition validity.
If instead \(p_2\)'s arms are an in-order sub-sequence of \(p_1\)'s, each surplus arm of \(p_1\) \Retire{}s (\cref{sec:idioms}).
Either way, a stable arm whose metadata changed picks up a \ChangeMeta{} of its own.
Alongside \Add{}s its position is immaterial, since no step in that emission waits on a guard.
Alongside \Retire{}s it goes first, since it shares nothing with the drains and would otherwise wait behind them.

\textbf{By shared label, in general.}
When neither list embeds in the other, the comparator pairs arms by the flows they carry rather than by position.
A leaf label identifies a unique flow across the whole policy, by \cref{sec:policy-dsl}'s leaf-partition validity.
A label shared between an arm of \(p_1\) and an arm of \(p_2\) is therefore evidence that one arm changed in place, while an arm whose labels appear on no opposing arm is fresh or vanishing.
This \emph{label-overlap walk} greedily pairs each \(p_1\) arm with the unique \(p_2\) arm with which it shares a label, then recurses on the pair; unpaired \(p_1\) arms are \Retire{}d and unpaired \(p_2\) arms are \Add{}ed.
The walk takes the first alignment that satisfies the label constraint, with no notion of cost; if no shared label yields even one pairing, it fails and the comparator gives up with a slot-level \Replace{}.
The walk is worth running even at equal arity.
The slot strategy pairs strictly by position, so an arm that has both moved to a new slot \emph{and} changed its subtree is compared against whatever now occupies its old slot, and the change reads as two unrelated edits rather than one.
The label walk instead follows the shared flow to the arm's new slot and sees the single change, so the comparator runs it alongside the slot walk and, whenever it exposes such a cross-slot pairing, takes it instead.

The trees do share structure.
\(g\) (\textsf{gmail}) in \(p_1\) and the nested \(Q\) in \(p_2\) share the label \textsf{gmail}, \textsf{drive} matches on both sides, and \textsf{slack} is the only arm whose labels appear nowhere in \(p_1\).
The walk reads this off directly, pairing \textsf{drive} with \textsf{drive} (no edit) and \textsf{gmail} with the nested \(Q\) (recurse), and treating \textsf{slack} as a pure add.
The emission is three steps: an \Add{} of \textsf{slack}, which the compiler can handle as a pure append; a \ChangeMeta{} that re-tunes \textsf{gmail}'s slot to \(m_4\); and a \Replace{} at that slot.

The metadata moves in two different ways as this fires.
\textsf{slack}'s requested metadatum \(m_3\) is carried by the \Add.
It is the metadatum the form carries (\cref{sec:add}), so the new arm lands already carrying \(m_3\).
The changing arm is handled differently, since its metadatum moves from \(m_2\) to \(m_4\).
Nothing in the \Replace{} can carry that change, so the slot needs a \ChangeMeta{} of its own, and it goes in front, since the \Replace{} takes the whole slot.
Had it gone last, every packet arriving for the incoming \(Q\) during the drain would stay ranked under \(m_2\) for the rest of its life.
The slot therefore carries \(m_4\) throughout the transient, which is what panel (c) shows, and \Designate{} itself still leaves the slot's metadatum alone (\cref{sec:designate}).
The example exhibits both behaviors: \(m_1\) and \(m_3\) come through untouched, while \(m_2\) is explicitly re-tuned to \(m_4\).

Panels (c) and (d) of \cref{fig:label-overlap-example} show the two policies the control passes through: first the transient \(\Strictstar\) that drains the old \textsf{gmail} leaf beside the incoming \(Q\), then the settled tree once that drain completes.
In the settled tree \textsf{slack} sits beside the rebuilt \textsf{gmail} subtree in an order that does not match \(p_2\) literally, but sibling order carries no semantics (\cref{sec:policy-dsl}), so the reordering is invisible to any observer.

\paragraph{Indexing a mixed emission}

When a comparison yields \Add{}s, \Retire{}s, and recursions at once, as the label-overlap walk can, the planner uses the reorder freedom of \cref{sec:policy-dsl} to keep the indexing trivial.
The \Add{}s are appended at the parent's tail.
The \Retire{}s fire at their original \(p_1\) indices in descending order, so a higher-index drain does not shift the lower indices still pending.
Each matched-pair recursion lands at its position among the matches, which is also its slot once the \Retire{}s drain.
Issuing the \Add{}s first is deliberate: an \Add{} is instantaneous while a \Retire{} waits for its subtree to drain, so the new arms begin carrying traffic immediately instead of waiting on the retirements.
A matched pair whose arms agree and whose metadata alone moved is issued first for the same reason, since its \ChangeMeta{} shares nothing with any of the drains and would otherwise wait behind them.
Such a step names the arm's index before the \Retire{}s rather than after, since that is where the arm sits when it fires.

\paragraph{Where the descent localizes the difference}

Every recursion descends through a slot where the two trees agree or where the label-overlap walk has paired them.
So by the time the per-level comparator gives up and emits a \Replace{} at depth \(k\), every shallower level has already matched a tight case.
The \Replace{} lands at the deepest slot to which the planner could isolate the difference, leaving every ancestor's other subtrees running undisturbed.
The slot-level \Replace{} is the \cref{sec:fallback} idiom with a non-empty path: it wraps only the slot's subtree, quiesces only that subtree, and rewires only the parent edge into it.
The worst case is the root-level \(\Replace([], p_2)\) of \cref{sec:fallback}, reached only when the per-level comparator gives up at the root with no recursive descent.
